# SITE 2026 — Session 12: Banking and the Deposit Franchise

**Dates:** Mon Aug 24 2026, 8:15am – Tue Aug 25 2026, 4:45pm PDT

Theme: reexamining the deposit franchise as a driver of banks' interest-rate risk, funding
stability, and monetary transmission — (i) measuring deposit stickiness, (ii) how banks build/maintain
deposit franchises (pricing, geography, tech, balance sheet), (iii) implications for risk-taking,
fragility, and regulation.

## Papers read — summaries

### 1. The Dynamics of Retail Deposit Balances (Palmer, Argyle, Iverson, Kotter, Nadauld)
12M credit-union accounts, 2011–2023. Retail deposits are extremely skewed (10% of
depositors hold 70% of balances), and that skew is entirely responsible for aggregate
deposit "stickiness": high-balance accounts appear via one large lump-sum inflow, sit for
a while, then get drawn down abruptly and idiosyncratically — and their size is *completely
insensitive* to interest rates (semi-elasticity ≈ 0 via 2SLS using FOMC surprises as an
instrument, confirmed via bunching-at-rate-notch tests). Low-balance accounts *are* rate
sensitive (semi-elasticity ≈ -7). Interpretation: large balances function as temporary
"liquidity holding bins" for lumpy expenditures (home down payments, tuition, etc.), not
as yield-seeking savings — a money-demand/liquidity-services story, not a market-power or
services story (both directly tested and rejected).

### 2. Banking on Inattention (Lu & Wu)
Theory + empirics on why banks can pay near-zero on deposits. Builds a dynamic
deposit-pricing model where depositors optimize infrequently (Poisson "attention" clock);
inattentive depositors are less sensitive to spreads, so banks profitably widen the spread
for them. Novel measure of inattention: half-life gap between how fast a depositor draws
down *scheduled* income (paycheck, ~6 days) vs *unscheduled* income (tax refund, ~25 days) —
argued to reflect inattention rather than consumption/liquidity-constraint differences.
Extends this measure to the whole US population via ML on IRS/Census data, aggregates to
bank level, and shows banks with more inattentive depositors: pay lower rates, have weaker
monetary pass-through, and see less rate-sensitive outflows. Calibrated model implies ~$2.6tn
aggregate deposit franchise value, and — the paper's most striking result — that deposit
franchise duration is *non-monotonic* in the policy rate: negative duration at low rates,
flipping positive at high rates, meaning banks' interest-rate risk exposure changes sign
over the cycle.

### 3. Bye Bye Beta: Deposit Duration with Fixed Spreads (Robert Rogers)
Challenges DeMarzo-Krishnamurthy-Nagel's claim that low rates always raise deposit-franchise
value. Fits a "fixed spread" model of deposit rates (deposit rate = risk-free rate minus a
constant, floored at zero) against call-report data 1987–2023 and finds it fits far better than
the standard "constant beta" model (R²=0.997 vs 0.93). Pricing deposits as interest-rate *caps*
(not linear liabilities, via a calibrated LIBOR market model) reveals franchise value is
hump-shaped in the rate level, peaking near 2% — duration actually turns *negative* at very low
rates even absent runoff or fixed costs. This duration measure correctly predicts the sign of
bank-stock reactions to FOMC surprises (2002–2023), where competing measures (DeMarzo et al.,
Begenau-Piazzesi-Schneider, Haddad-Sraer) get the sign wrong — and explains banks' 2020-21
scramble into long-duration MBS as a hedge against persistently low rates.

### 4. What Makes Depositors Tick? (Cirelli & Olafsson)
Uses Icelandic bank data (near-frictionless environment: instant free transfers between
checking/savings/money-market, full digital banking, no joint accounts) to study liquid
asset allocation where transfer frictions are essentially absent. Even here, most households
barely reallocate toward higher-yielding accounts when spreads widen — inaction persists even
without any switching cost. Wealthy households are ~10x more rate-sensitive than the median
household and drive nearly all the aggregate deposit reallocation seen at the bank level.
Financial literacy and inflation-awareness (from linked surveys) predict who's rate-sensitive
within the wealth distribution. A calibrated Baumol-Tobin-style adjustment-friction model
matches the cross-sectional *level* of holdings well but badly overstates rate-sensitivity —
suggesting inattention/behavioral frictions matter beyond simple adjustment costs.

### 5. How Do Banks Compete? Evidence from Advertising Videos (Chen, Hu, Ma)
A creative-data paper: 51,349 Nielsen TV ad clips (2004–2020) run through an unsupervised
video-embedding pipeline (multimodal Qwen2.5-Omni-7B embeddings → clustering) to classify bank
ad content into pricing (18%), service-quality (29%), and trust/emotion (53%) categories with no
human labels. High-market-share banks systematically downplay pricing in favor of service/trust
messaging; new branch entrants lean on pricing and trust; M&A entrants (inheriting an existing
customer base) ramp up service messaging instead (+7.8pp). Ad content tracks fundamentals:
pricing-heavy banks have higher deposit betas (weaker market power), service/trust-heavy banks
have wider deposit spreads and amplify the deposits channel of monetary transmission — confirmed
causally via a DMA-border-discontinuity design. First direct evidence that a specific, budgeted
bank action (advertising) actively manufactures deposit-franchise value.

### 7. Deposit Specialization and Lending Behavior (Blickle, Parlatore, Saunders)
NY Fed Staff Report using four confidential supervisory datasets (FR 2052a, Y-14 loan-level
C&I data, Y-14 securities, Y-9C) on the ~25 largest US banks. Classifies banks by dominant
depositor type — retail, corporate, or NBFI — and shows this specialization is stark and
persistent (retail banks: 60%+ retail deposits, near-zero NBFI), maps directly onto assets:
retail-funded banks hold more C&I/real-estate loans at lower rates and longer maturities
(25th→75th percentile retail-deposit-share adds ~7 quarters of loan maturity); NBFI-funded
banks hold almost purely cash/Treasuries. The COVID deposit surge (a quasi-exogenous liquidity
shock) shows heterogeneous, specialization-dependent deployment: retail banks parked new money
in real estate/cash, corporate banks converted inflows into C&I lending, NBFI banks did almost
nothing — with real consequences for borrowing firms' growth. A new asset-duration/liability-
maturity risk index flags SVB as a clear pre-failure outlier, validating it as an early-warning
tool. Bottom line: banks are not interchangeable conduits for monetary policy — *which* bank
receives a liquidity injection matters for real-economy outcomes.

### 8. Bank Market Power (Bocola, Bornstein, Malgieri, Puglisi)
Flips the deposit-franchise lens to the *lending* side. Uses AnaCredit (euro-area credit
register) and a longer Italian panel (2005–2019) to run an AKM-style two-way fixed-effects
decomposition of loan rates (firm FE + bank FE), finding bank fixed effects explain 15–20% of
rate dispersion — about half the explanatory power of default risk/collateral — and this rose
as the industry consolidated post-crisis (top-5 local market share 57%→78%). Bank fixed effects
correlate strongly and robustly with local market share, confirming a genuine market-power (not
cost-heterogeneity) story. A calibrated oligopoly GE model (loan-demand elasticity ≈ -19) shows
eliminating markups entirely raises output 1.3% short-run but only ~0.4-0.5% long-run, since
lower bank profits erode capital and tighten leverage over time. Also: halving capital
requirements lowers loan rates by up to 83bp, with about half the effect coming from reduced
*markups* (share reallocation from large to small banks) rather than lower funding costs — an
underappreciated pro-competitive channel of capital regulation.

### 9. The Consumer Welfare Effects of US Bank Mergers (Pakes, Whinston, Zheng)
Asks whether bank-merger antitrust review, which focuses on price effects, is missing the
bigger channel: branch closures. Combines FDIC branch-level data, RateWatch rates, and a
structural BLP-style discrete-choice model of small-business relationship formation and
consumer deposit supply across nearby branches, studying 55 US counties with exactly one 2015
bank merger vs. 2,565 no-merger counties. Distance to a branch matters enormously (1% closer
branch → +4.1% small-business relationship formation, +2.4% consumer deposits — equivalent to a
1.6% deposit-rate increase for the median consumer). Post-merger, merged banks cut branch growth
18% and deposit growth 7%, while rate changes were small/insignificant. Overall consumer welfare
fell 8.9% in merger counties vs. 0.2% in non-merger counties (an 8.7pp gap, equivalent to a 35%
rate cut) — and branch-network effects (4.5pp) are roughly *twice* the size of the rate-driven
effect (2.8pp). Direct evidence that branch proximity, not just the posted rate, is a first-order
component of deposit-franchise value, with clear implications for DOJ/Fed merger review.

### 10. Market-Priced Savings, Bank Deposit Market Power, and Monetary Policy Transmission (Basten, Cucic, Schepens)
Danish administrative data linking every depositor's bank accounts to their *entire*
investment portfolio (stocks/bonds/funds = "market-priced savings," MPS). Banks
individually price-discriminate: within the same bank-year, MPS holders get 6.6bps more
pass-through per 100bps policy hike than otherwise-identical non-holders — a depositor-level,
within-bank version of the deposits-channel story (distinct from cross-bank market-power
variation à la Drechsler-Savov-Schnabl). Uses account fixed effects, entry/exit event
studies, and unexpected-parental-death inheritances (clean natural experiment: heirs who
inherit stocks/bonds vs. only cash/real-estate) to rule out confounds like wealth,
sophistication, or attention — MPS premium tracks *active* portfolio ownership, not innate
traits. Despite the extra pass-through, MPS holders still withdraw more (2.4pp more per
100bps), and banks with more MPS-heavy depositor bases cut lending more when rates rise
(Khwaja-Mian firm-level design). Euro-area cross-country evidence corroborates the mechanism.

### 11. The Declining Role of Deposits in Credit Creation (Koont & Walz)
Decomposes the total effect of a deposit inflow on bank credit into (i) banks' direct marginal
lending response and (ii) an indirect "credit-deposit multiplier" feedback loop, in which loan
proceeds circulate and partly recycle back into stable deposits that fund further lending. Using
high-frequency Treasury-cash-settlement timing as an instrument for deposit inflows (weekly
data, 2005-2019) plus a structural model matched to the reduced-form multiplier, finds the
total bank-credit effect of $1 of deposits has fallen from $2.13 to $0.88 over two decades —
driven by both a weaker direct credit response (0.88→0.58, reflecting higher balance-sheet/
capital costs) and a lower recycling rate (0.65→0.47, reflecting declining deposit "specialness"
as savers substitute into money-market funds and other non-bank alternatives). Central bank
policy matters too: QE lowers the marginal credit impact of a deposit (balance-sheet expansion
tightens the capital constraint), while QT can raise it (scarcer liquidity raises the marginal
value of stable funding). Non-bank credit does NOT offset the decline — a deposit inflow that
reaches non-banks only via lower bank loan rates actually *contracts* non-bank credit rather
than expanding it, and non-bank intermediation chains are too long for leaked flows to make up
the gap. Bottom line: an "implicit narrowing" of banking — deposits remain integrated with
lending but have become much less effective at amplifying credit creation, with implications
for monetary/fiscal transmission and for how much a shift toward stablecoins or other non-bank
money substitutes would actually matter for aggregate credit supply.

### 12. Interest Rate Risk and Cross-Sectional Effects of Micro-Prudential Regulation (Begenau, Elenev, Landvoigt)
Builds a two-period GE model of heterogeneous banks that jointly choose interest-rate risk
(securities portfolios) and funding mix (insured vs. differentiated uninsured deposits) with
endogenous depositor runs, calibrated to FFIEC call-report data 2010–2022. Reproduces the
empirically observed U-shaped securities share and rising uninsured-deposit concentration in
bank size purely from differential deposit-market power and productivity — no too-big-to-fail
assumption needed (though adding it amplifies large-bank risk-taking further). Small banks fail
mainly from solvency shocks (~1.5% probability); large banks' failures are run-driven (up to ~5%
in bad states, doubling to ~10% after an unanticipated rate hike, vs. only +10bp for small
banks). Counterintuitively, when large banks rationally anticipate more rate volatility they hold
*fewer* precautionary bonds, increasing fragility. Among policy tools, a size-dependent capital
requirement matches an economy-wide capital hike's run-risk reduction while staying close to
welfare-neutral (leaving small banks' leverage undisturbed) — outperforming uniform capital or
liquidity rules, which trade off run-risk reduction against lending/liquidity provision. Directly
informs the post-2023 debate on regulating large regional/systemic banks' rate risk.
