## Papers read — summaries

### 1. Regulatory Arbitrage within the Firm (Cetorelli & Kundu)
When Basel III (2015) tightened capital rules for bank subsidiaries but not for their nonbank
affiliates, US bank holding companies (BHCs) did not raise outside equity. They pulled equity out
of lightly regulated nonbank subsidiaries and moved it into their banks. Organizational complexity
is instrumented with the timing of each state's interstate-banking deregulation (1970s–90s). Banks
in BHCs from states that deregulated one year earlier hold 0.74pp more excess capital after 2015,
about 5pp at the median 7-year gap. Consolidated equity, assets, and lending do not change, so the
money moved inside the firm rather than coming from outside. The mechanism shows up directly in
parent–subsidiary equity flows:
- Nonbanks pay much larger dividends up to the parent.
- The parent redirects its equity investments toward the banks.
- The banks keep their earnings.
- Nonbanks are charged about 300bp more for internal funding.
- Outside equity issuance falls and buybacks rise.

Nonbanks act as "equity reservoirs": the median one holds 16% of consolidated equity on 2% of
assets. After 2015 banks become safer and more profitable. Nonbanks become thinner, riskier, and
push into consumer lending, and their distance-to-default falls. In stress tests at 2008-crisis
scale, 4–6% of BHCs would exhaust their capital buffers if they recapitalized their nonbanks. For
tail-risk firms, a 50% pass-through of nonbank losses back to the bank erodes 72% of the apparent
safety gain. Bottom line: risk moves inside the firm rather than disappearing, and consolidated
supervision misses it.

### 2. The Optimal Use of AI in Financial Regulation (Clayton & Coppola)
The authors build a "graph transformer", an attention-augmented graph neural network. It learns
embeddings of investors and assets from the network of overlapping portfolios: about 37,000
non-bank intermediaries, 85,000 assets, and roughly $40tn of holdings (FactSet/Morningstar-based
security-level data). Given the cross-section of holdings and an aggregate stress level, it
predicts each position's change. In walk-forward out-of-sample tests it beats balance-sheet,
econometric, and other deep-learning benchmarks at forecasting trading, including in crises. It
also generalizes to asset classes or investors held out of training entirely. The embeddings were
never trained on prices, yet they explain crisis-period bond returns: +18pp of incremental
adjusted R², against about 1pp for the best traditional low-dimensional fire-sale measures. One
interpretable dimension, crowding among flow-sensitive holders × illiquidity, accounts for about a
quarter of that. As a systemic-risk metric, predicted selling beats ΔCoVaR, outflows, and
concentration. The theory addresses the Lucas critique: the optimal intervention is τ* = Ω·L̂,
where Ω is the causal policy impact (structural) and L̂ is predicted forced selling (predictive).
So a purely predictive model can raise welfare by sharpening where to intervene, and a weighted
predictive R² is a sufficient statistic for its welfare value. Prediction and structural knowledge
are complements.

### 3. Credit Commitments by Nonbanks (Huang & Xu)
Using hand-collected facility-level data on business development companies (BDCs), the authors
show that these nonbank private-credit lenders now extend large revolvers and delayed-draw
commitments. By 2025Q4 BDC commitment-to-asset ratios are comparable to banks'. That challenges
the Kashyap–Rajan–Stein view that liquidity insurance is the banks' preserve. BDC commitment books
are concentrated by industry, geography, and sponsor, and draws are correlated. A typical BDC's
liquidity-shock volatility is about 2.5× that of a diversified benchmark, and a 1-SD shock would
wipe out its cash in about a fifth of BDC-quarters. BDCs back this up with undrawn bank credit
lines rather than cash. They draw on those lines and expand them when they need liquidity, and the
lines held up even in the 2023 regional-bank stress. This is the first data documentation of the
"dash for liquidity" chain that FSOC has flagged. A layered Holmström–Tirole model shows why a BDC
rations its insurance: it faces undiversified risk and has no public backstop. The data match
this: each $1 of unfunded commitments carries only about $0.53 of bank-line slack. Rationing
creates a tragedy-of-the-commons externality in which borrower firms underinvest in their own
liquidity management. The inefficiency is specific to nonbank liquidity provision.

### 4. Real Origins of Financial Structure (Buchak & Jiang)
The paper argues that the bank-vs-market split is partly a demand-side outcome of the real
economy. Across countries, economies with wealthier and older households rely less on bank
deposits, and those with more service-heavy and intangible-intensive firms rely less on bank loans.
A 1-SD rise in wealth per capita goes with about 6pp lower deposit share. Both patterns survive
institutional controls. The authors then estimate an equilibrium model:
- Heterogeneous households choose among deposits, money market funds (MMFs), bonds, equity, and
  bank vs. nonbank loans (disciplined with the Survey of Consumer Finances).
- Firms choose liquid assets and bank vs. market debt (Census Quarterly Financial Report data).
- Banks compete monopolistically under capital and liquidity wedges, alongside competitive MMFs
  and nonbank lenders.

Holding intermediary technology and regulation fixed at 2019, moving the real sector from its 1983
to its 2019 distribution lowers the bank funding share by 5.5pp and the bank liquidity ratio by
17.4pp, while leaving the bank credit share roughly flat (−0.5pp). Almost all of this comes from
the household side (wealth, aging, concentration). Firm heterogeneity mainly changes the mix of
credit demand. Real-sector demand explains a meaningful part of cross-country deposit reliance but
less of the credit side, where institutions still matter. This gives a demand-side foundation for
the growth of private credit and PE.

### 5. The Elasticity of Home Purchases to Financing Costs (Kim, Liu, Zhang)
The authors exploit the May 2023 overhaul of GSE loan-level price adjustments, which moved
guarantee fees (g-fees) differently at FICO cutoffs (640/680/720/760/780). The design is a stacked
difference-in-differences just above vs. just below each cutoff, using confidential HMDA data for
above-median-income buyers. A 100bp g-fee cut lowers the fee-adjusted GSE rate by 28–33bp and
raises relative home purchases by 13.9%. That is a 21–25% semi-elasticity per 50bp, 1.5–1.8× the
Bhutta–Ringo FHA estimate. About half works outside debt-to-income (DTI) constraints, which is
new. The elasticity more than doubles from the loosest to the tightest quartile of housing
markets (by days on market). A calibrated search-and-matching housing model gives much smaller
aggregate elasticities (up to 16.4% per 100bp), because in tight markets differential pricing
mostly changes *who* buys. Counterfactuals:
- A uniform +25bp g-fee cuts GSE volume 3–6% but raises GSE profits by about $1.3bn a year. The
  GSEs can therefore rebuild capital ahead of leaving conservatorship.
- The 2023 reform itself moved pricing toward risk, with small aggregate and income-distribution
  effects, despite the media outcry.

### 6. Household Migration and Collateral Constraint (He, Liu, Pang, Su, Zou)
China's 2015–18 shantytown renovation program paid more than ¥4tn in cash for illiquid shanty
houses. The authors combine Census micro-data with a Bartik-style measure of the cash that flows
into each destination city along pre-2015 migration networks. House prices did not rise faster in
the cities that paid out the cash. They rose in destination cities receiving the cash, especially
from lower-priced origin cities (about 3× larger effects for high-price-gap pairs). Two
mechanisms: pre-existing migrants were less likely to "downgrade" back home, and new migrants were
more likely to "upgrade" to pricier cities. A dynamic spatial GE model with collateral constraints,
shanty-vs-normal housing, and cross-city homeownership compares cash with a local-only voucher.
The housing-spending multiplier is 1.14–1.18 under cash vs. 0.80–0.84 under vouchers, so
endogenous migration amplifies the response by about 40%. The program explains more than 20% of
2016–20 house-price growth. Lesson: the borrowing constraint measured where households live today
understates the "shadow" constraint in the city they want to move to.

### 7. Buying from the Family: PE-Owned Insurers and Their Affiliated Investments (Huber, Huber, Shan, Zhu)
The authors map every US insurer's ownership chain (NAIC Schedule Y plus PE deal data) and link it
to security-level holdings and trades (Schedules D/BA) and to NAIC CLO stress tests. PE-owned
insurers went from under $1bn of assets in 2009 to $696bn in 2024. They put more than 40% of new
investments into structured securities, over 60% of it issued by affiliates, against under 2% for
independent insurers. Within those affiliated deals, PE-owned insurers:
- buy harder-to-value paper (greater dispersion in arm's-length fair values);
- pay more for the same security on the same day (47bp more in private primary deals);
- impair and sell underwater affiliated positions far less often (offsetting about 80% of the
  normal affiliation-driven impairment response).

These investments do not perform better. They show lower arm's-length fair values within two years
and bigger projected stress losses. Under a 2008-type scenario, affiliated holdings add about 4pp
of portfolio loss beyond baseline, more than half of PE-owned insurers' 7.6% equity buffer.
Integrating capital supply and demand inside one conglomerate weakens arm's-length discipline and
creates a new channel from private credit to policyholders and to fixed-income markets.

### 8. Bank Runs With and Without Bank Failure (Correia, Luck, Verner)
LLMs applied to digitized historical newspapers build a database of 3,984 runs on individual US
banks (1863–1934), plus 13,772 suspensions and 10,341 failures. The data are validated against
known crisis chronologies and balance-sheet deposit drops, with about a 95% match on ground-truth
samples. Runs are much more likely at weak banks (low capital, noncore funding). Strong banks also
get run after bad macro or banking news, such as stock declines, local business failures, or runs
on neighbors. A run raises the annual failure probability by 38pp, but most runs do *not* end in
failure. In the weakest decile, 59% of run banks fail; in the strongest decile almost none do.
Survivors signal strength (equity injections), borrow from other banks, get clearinghouse help,
or suspend temporarily (28% of non-failure runs). Of 53 "non-fundamental" (misinformation) runs,
only 11% end in failure, and only at weak or fraudulent banks. Surviving banks lose about 7% of
assets in deposits and loans for four years or more. City-level credit and manufacturing fall
(about 5%) only after runs on weak banks. Conclusion: fundamentals, not self-fulfilling panics,
drive both failure and real damage. The authors argue this tempers the Diamond–Dybvig view that
small shocks trigger bad equilibria.

### 9. Competing for Loan Informal Seniority (Martins, Ricca, Taburet)
Brazil's credit register lets the authors see each borrower's loans across all lenders. 46.5% of
85M card users have cards from two or more issuers. Among borrowers who default on at least one
card, 84% keep paying another; with two cards, 76% default on only one. Selective defaulters
prioritize:
- cards with higher limits (+4pp);
- cards from digital banks (+14pp);
- cards from lenders that also hold their mortgage (+14pp) or auto loan (+5pp).

Cross-selling therefore buys implicit seniority. A payroll-bank-partnership difference-in-
differences shows that one more card lender raises default by about 10pp, which is evidence of debt
dilution. In the model, lenders compete for repayment priority through "relationship benefits."
Debt dilution pushes toward underprovision and the seniority race pushes toward overprovision.
A sufficient-statistic calibration puts credit limits 7% below first-best (−9.5% from dilution,
+2.5% from the race). Equal-recovery bankruptcy rules would remove the race and cut limits a
further 2.5%, so standard bankruptcy features can *reduce* welfare.

### 12. Financial Supermarkets? Cross-Selling in Retail Banking (Cao & Wang)
The authors use SCF, MRI-Simmons, and an original Prolific survey that records consideration sets,
switching histories, and choice experiments. About 35% of consumers with one checking account and
one card hold both at the same bank, against 6.4% under independent choice. The ratio is far higher
at regional banks (over 60× vs. about 6× at megabanks) and holds across all credit-score segments,
so bank information about borrowers does not explain it. A dynamic structural model of deposit and
credit-card choice under limited consideration (estimated by Monte Carlo EM) separates three forces:
correlated preferences, consideration spillovers, and utility complementarity. Findings:
- Rewards cards are about 16% of accounting profits, but removing them costs banks about 26% of
  profits. They are a loss leader that keeps high-income depositors.
- A deposit-only "narrow bank" entrant is at a steep disadvantage.
- Interchange-fee caps spill over into deposit competition.
- A Citi + super-regional merger flagged by a deposit-HHI screen becomes pro-competitive once
  cross-selling is modeled: rivals *raise* deposit rates.

Implication: product-by-product merger and regulatory analysis is misleading.

### 13. How Do Banks Compete? Evidence from Advertising Videos (Chen, Hu, Ma)
Newer (88-page) draft of Session 12 #5. The data are 51,349 Nielsen TV ad clips (2004–2020, 2,739
institutions, 210 local TV markets (DMAs)), classified by unsupervised multimodal video embeddings
into pricing (18%), service (29%), and trust/emotion (53%). New in this version is a formal model.
Pricing ads raise depositors' rate attention, while service ads (informative) and trust ads
(persuasive) draw attention away from rates. High-reach banks therefore run a deposit-market "fat
cat" strategy: they displace pricing content and soften price competition. With sleepy depositors,
this lowers deposit betas. Empirically:
- An interquartile rise in local mortgage market share goes with 2.5% fewer pricing mentions.
- Organic branch entrants push pricing and trust; M&A entrants push service; incumbents answer
  fintech entry with service content.
- Wells Fargo raised trust content about 14% after its 2016 scandal.
- Content lines up with fundamentals (rates, complaint ratios).
- Service-heavy banks widen deposit spreads more and shrink more when the Fed hikes.
- A DMA-border discontinuity design shows ad content causally moves demand for deposits,
  mortgages, and small-business loans.

### 14. Household Portfolio and Deposit Insurance (Ghosh, Limodio, Vats)
In a portfolio model, a limited deposit-insurance (DI) cap kinks the capital allocation line.
Depositors bunch at the cap and hold more risky assets than they would with full insurance. The
authors test this on a 4% sample of depositors at a large Indian private bank, whose accounts are
linked to stock, mutual-fund, loan, and family data. India raised the DI limit from ₹1 lakh to
₹5 lakh in February 2020. In a bunching-in-differences design with depositor and ZIP×month fixed
effects, bunchers raise deposits 3.6–5.1% relative to non-bunchers (a 2.1–3.1% elasticity per pp
of coverage). They fund this by selling stocks and mutual funds, about 72% of the increase,
identified with ISIN×time fixed effects. They especially unwind state-owned-enterprise stocks,
which they had used as a "quasi-guaranteed" substitute, with a price dip that reverts within a
month. The share of bunchers is a sufficient statistic for depositors' perceived bank-failure
probability: about 0.54% at risk aversion 3, 1.74% at 10. Welfare rises at least 0.04% even after
bank moral hazard.
