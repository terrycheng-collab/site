## Papers read — summaries

### 1. Measuring the Option Value of Change (Chassang, Chen, Kolesár)
When treatment effects are heterogeneous across units but persistent over time, a dynamic rule
("try it, keep it only where early results look good") has option value. The econometric catch: if
estimation errors are auto-correlated, a naive plug-in that replaces true effects with estimated ones
confuses correlated noise for correlated effects and overstates the gain. The paper shows how to
estimate the value of dynamic adoption rules in observational data using assumptions on the time-series
process for outcomes (latent Markov state with no long-run effect of dropping treatment, or an
autoregressive model), plus a placebo-based test. Applied to a newly built dataset of US adoptions of
Operation Ceasefire–style gang-violence programs (122 rollouts counted, 1996–2018): the average effect is a statistically
insignificant 0.76 fewer homicides per 100k (baseline ≈15.7). Dropping the program where first-two-year
estimates are unfavorable raises the effect to 1.82–2.43 per treatment year (significant under one
model), whereas the naive estimator would claim 3.02–4.49. So selective reporting of success stories
(as the program's promoters do) isn't entirely misleading, but the transparent number to report is the
value of dynamic adoption.

### 2. Synthetic Blips: Generalizing Synthetic Controls for Dynamic Treatment Effects (Agarwal, Han, Saha, Syrgkanis, Yoon)
Extends synthetic controls / synthetic interventions to settings with many sequential, adaptively
assigned treatments that depend on a latent, time-varying confounding state. Assuming a low-rank
latent-factor model for "blip" effects (a structural nested mean model with low-rank structure), each
unit's per-period blip effect is expressed recursively, by backward induction, as a linear combination
of other units' blip effects — avoiding the combinatorial explosion of donor units that a naive
synthetic-intervention approach would need. Covers time-varying and time-invariant linear dynamical
systems as special cases, trades off sample complexity against how adaptive the assignment policy can
be, and delivers PCR-based estimators with asymptotic theory. Application: Korean firm panel
(2,052 firms, 2006–2015) receiving export insurance (K-SURE) and/or export loans (EXIM). Insurance pays
off only from year 4; loans hurt for two years then catch up (working-capital channel); cumulative
five-year ATEs ≈ 85 bn KRW (insurance) vs 34 bn (loans). Timing matters: back-loading support beats
spreading it thin, and firm-level estimates feed retrospective and decision-tree prospective targeting
rules that raise exports with less public spending.

### 3. Inference for Treatment Effects Conditional on Generalized Principal Strata using IV (Bai, Huang, Moon, Santos, Shaikh, Vytlacil)
A unifying framework for discrete treatments and instruments with general outcomes: parameters are
expectations of any function of the *response type* (the full vector of potential outcomes and
potential treatments) conditional on a "generalized principal stratum" (a set of response types).
Restrictions rule certain response types out (probability zero) — nesting LATE monotonicity, one-sided
noncompliance, Kirkeboen-Kline-Walters and Heckman-Pinto revealed-preference restrictions, and Manski-
Pepper monotone treatment response. Key result: the identified set (and the model's testable
implications) is characterized by existence of a nonnegative solution to a specially structured linear
system; building on Fang et al. (2023), with improved coupling rates, they give tests and confidence
sets that are uniformly valid even when the number of response types is large relative to the sample.
Application to Blattman et al.'s (2017) Liberia CBT+cash RCT (n=999): no short-term complementarity,
but in the long run at least ~5% of the population would stop selling drugs with therapy+cash rather
than cash alone, with larger effects among pairwise compliers.

### 4. From Unstructured Data to Demand Counterfactuals (Christensen & Compiani)
Demand models increasingly proxy the dimensions of product differentiation with ML embeddings of text or
images; if the proxies miss the true dimensions driving substitution, counterfactuals are biased and
standard errors invalid. The key reframing is that this is *misspecification*, not classical measurement
error (the mismeasurement is at the product level, the observations at the market/individual level).
They reparameterize the model around a composite parameter (how proxies interact with structural
parameters in utility) and derive a closed-form, post-estimation bias correction for counterfactuals that
is also semiparametrically efficient, plus analytic standard errors and LM-statistic diagnostics for how
many/which proxies to include. It works with BLP-style market data (with or without micro data) and
individual-choice models with product fixed effects, and allows proxies from ML models fine-tuned on the
same data. Using first-/second-choice experimental data, the correction raises the hit rate for predicting a
product's closest substitute from 40% to 70%, and the diagnostics pick the best-performing proxy set.

### 6. Bidding for Reputation (Jingyi Cui)
Freelancer.com data: 3M wage bids by 100k+ international workers on 83k jobs (2018). Employers value
good reviews and experience; new workers bid low and raise bids after good reviews. The model is a dynamic
equilibrium auction with persistent unobserved worker types, symmetric learning about latent quality,
and human-capital accumulation. It is estimated by extending two-step dynamic-game methods: an EM
algorithm handles continuous actions, multidimensional unobserved types, and a win-probability
transition that depends on rivals' types, with exchange rates as instruments for endogenous USD bids,
then MSM on forward-simulated continuation values. Five good reviews are worth a 22–24pp wage premium,
about half from expected quality and half from human capital. A first review's value goes 35% to the
worker and 65% to future employers. Forward-looking bidding raises the number of reviewed workers by
52% and quadruples matches relative to myopic bidding, but investment is still below the social
optimum. The optimal platform-funded subsidy for hiring new workers raises total surplus 22% (and
platform profit); the profit-maximizing subsidy captures 80% of that gain.

### 7. Statistical Inference of Optimal Allocations I (Feng, Hong, Nekipelov)
Theory for inference on the *value* of plug-in optimal treatment allocation rules (threshold a
first-stage CATE/regression estimate), where the indicator function makes standard delta-method
arguments fail. Using Hausdorff measure and area/coarea formulas from geometric measure theory, they
prove Hadamard differentiability of the "social welfare potential function" (the value function of
multi-class optimal allocation) in both the weights and the conditional mean functions. That gives
functional-delta-method asymptotics and bootstrap validity for binary resource-constrained allocation
and the plug-in ROC curve. They note that the value function is convex in the policy inputs, so its
first-order derivative with respect to the policy is degenerate. Combined with DML, this yields a
debiased value-function estimator, and the same regularity conditions justify the margin assumption
behind fast plug-in regret rates.

### 8. Testing Inequalities Linear in Nuisance Parameters (Cox, Shi, Shimizu)
Tests H₀: ∃δ with Cδ ≤ b, where C and b are estimated and δ is a partially identified nuisance
vector — covering subvector inference and specification tests in linear moment (in)equality models and
inference on parameters bounded by linear programs (e.g., MTR/PRTE bounds). Builds on Cox-Shi (2023):
first, an elementary closed-form formula for the data-dependent chi-squared degrees of freedom (which
shows the sCC test is a Sargan-Hansen J-test on the active inequalities); second, the new GCC test
handles an *estimated* Jacobian under a "stable rank" condition (weaker than full-rank strong ID), with a
two-step, GMM-like first stage to get the variance right. No tuning parameters, no simulation, uniform
asymptotic validity. Re-running Kline-Tartari (2016) Connecticut Jobs First bounds gives confidence
intervals comparable to theirs without manual elimination of nuisance parameters (4 seconds for all nine
intervals).

### 9. Inference for Linear Systems with Unknown Coefficients (Bai, Ponomarev, Tabord-Meehan, Santos, Shaikh, Torgovitsky)
Tests whether A₀x₀ + A₁x₁ = β has a solution with x₁ ≥ 0 when *all* of A₀, A₁, β are estimated, as in
NPIV with shape restrictions, random-coefficient average marginal effects, MTE/MTR bounds, synthetic
parallel trends with convex weights, and distribution-free binary-choice counterfactuals. They show that
testing can be literally impossible in some cases (the null is dense in total variation), so they
characterize the null's closure via Farkas' lemma as linear inequalities on the projection of (A₁, β) off
A₀'s column span. Sample splitting then gives a practical test: one half picks the most-violating
direction (two LPs), the other gives a closed-form statistic with a normal critical value. It is valid
under weak rank conditions with dimensions growing with n, which is weaker than the rank conditions in
Cox et al. (2025) and Goff-Mbakop (2025). "Direct" and "screening" variants are offered; screening gives
shorter CIs at the cost of one tuning parameter.

### 10. Production Function Estimation without Invertibility (Doraszelski & Li)
The OP/LP/ACF proxy approach requires that firms with equal productivity make equal input choices. That
fails with demand shocks, imperfect competition with unobserved rivals, unobserved conduct changes, or
input-price heterogeneity. If invertibility fails, productivity is a hidden Markov state, so lags of
observables should help predict it; that makes invertibility testable, and it is strongly rejected in
Spanish manufacturing firms and US manufacturing industries. The resulting first-step prediction error
invalidates capital as an instrument and biases step 2. They derive a necessary and sufficient condition
for the step-2 moment to still hold. In practice, include every step-2 instrument in the step-1
regression (e.g., add the *lead* of capital). This gives a first-order bias correction even when the
condition fails. A modified moment is Neyman-orthogonal, so step 2 is robust to step-1 noise and
ML-friendly, and a sensitivity diagnostic, dθ/dλ, measures exposure to the step-1 prediction error.
Monte Carlo evidence shows big gains.

### 11. Tractable Identification of Strategic Network Formation Models with Unobserved Heterogeneity (Gao, Li, Xu)
Combines two things the literature had kept apart: strategic link interdependence (endogenous network
statistics such as common friends) and individual fixed effects (degree heterogeneity). A "bounding-by-c"
trick treats endogenous covariates as random variables and uses monotonicity to get inequalities that
hold whatever equilibrium is played, so the intractable, multiple-equilibria map from primitives to
networks never has to be solved. A menu of subnetwork configurations follows. Tetrads difference out all
fixed effects. Triads and weighted restrictions difference out some and profile over the rest. General
weighted cycles unify these. With logistic shocks and a comparison-pattern invariance condition (satisfied
by common friends and Jaccard), a conditioned tetrad log-odds ratio *point* identifies the parameters,
generalizing Graham's (2017) tetrad logit to strategic settings. Preliminary simulations show informative
bounds; formal inference and an application are left for future work.

### 12. Identification of Structural Parameters in Dynamic Discrete Choice Games with Fixed Effects (Aguirregabiria, Gu, Mira) — *read from the March 2021 draft*
With short panels (large M markets, small T), misspecified unobserved heterogeneity biases both switching-cost
parameters (spurious state dependence) and strategic-interaction parameters (common shocks look like
peer/competition effects). The paper extends Chamberlain/Honoré-Kyriazidou sufficient-statistic
conditional likelihood to dynamic games, handling myopic vs forward-looking players, complete vs
incomplete information, and triangular (Stackelberg) vs full contemporaneous interaction, all with
fixed effects that are left unrestricted along with initial conditions. Results (by proposition): in myopic
triangular games, the competition effect γ₂ is identified iff own or cross switching costs vanish.
Where conditional likelihood fails, Bonhomme-style functional differencing identifies more, e.g., γ₂ and β₁₂
when the two players share a fixed effect. With multiple equilibria, they combine sufficient statistics
for *bounds* on history probabilities, which they describe as the first combination of fixed-effects
sufficient statistics with partial identification. That gives point identification of switching costs
and bounds on strategic effects in myopic games and partial identification of switching costs with
forward-looking players. The 2021 draft's estimation, empirical (price competition), and conclusion
sections are still "TBW"; the April 2026 program version adds the application, but only its title page
is public.
