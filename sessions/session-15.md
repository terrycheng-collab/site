# SITE 2026 — Session 15: Causes and Consequences of Misallocation: Theory and Empirics

**Dates:** Mon Aug 31 2026, 12:00pm – Tue Sep 1 2026, 5:15pm PDT
**Location:** Stanford Graduate School of Business, P107, 655 Knight Way, Stanford, CA 94305
**Organizers:** Michael Blank (Stanford), Pete Klenow (Stanford), Adrien Matray (Atlanta Fed), Sara Moreira (Northwestern)
**Source:** https://economics.stanford.edu/events/site-2026/session-15-causes-and-consequences-misallocation-theory-and-empirics

Theme: how frictions and distortions drive wedges in marginal products across firms, and what that
costs in aggregate productivity, growth, and welfare. The program runs in four blocks: (i) market power
and markups (spatial, establishment-level, demand-driven, R&D), (ii) innovation (pricing frictions, public
R&D, organizational frictions), (iii) firm dynamics (inventor teams, entrant expectations), and (iv)
incomplete markets (intangibles and borrowing, risk-sharing).

## Papers read — summaries

### 1. The Spatial Distribution of Markups (Becker, Edmond, Midrigan, Xu)
A quantitative spatial model with multi-establishment firms and Atkeson–Burstein oligopoly, so markups
vary by firm and destination. It is calibrated to about 220,000 US manufacturing firms (363 six-digit
NAICS sectors, about 600 firms per sector) across 170 BEA Economic Areas. Every firm's establishments
sit exactly where they are in the Census data, and sector-specific iceberg costs reproduce Commodity
Flow Survey gravity regressions. Intranational trade frictions are the key force: they limit
competition so much that a model without geography only matches the benchmark's aggregate markup and
concentration after 90% of its firms are randomly removed (about 60 per sector). Without geography,
doubling or halving the number of firms barely moves markups. The aggregate markup is 1.26, ranging
from 1.22 in New York to 1.35 in Honolulu. The welfare cost of markups is 5.8% on average, against
3.7% without geography, and it is highly unequal: about 1% in New York versus up to 20% in Honolulu.
Cutting trade costs 20% lowers markups roughly four times more in remote areas and raises
consumption about ten times more (about 1.6% in New York, nearly 16% in the rural Dakotas). It also
produces the observed divergence of rising national and falling local concentration, so rising
national concentration can coincide with *more* competition and less misallocation.

### 2. More Than the Sum of Its Parts? Markups and the Role of Establishments (Kondziella & Weiss)
Uses the universe of Swedish services firms and establishments (1997–2017) to document three facts.
Multi-establishment firms are rare but earn half of sales. A firm's markup rises with sales *per
establishment* but not with its number of establishments, so market power is set at the
establishment level. Each successive establishment at a firm is smaller relative to its local
market. The authors build a model of competition through establishments with Kimball demand at
the establishment level and prove that firms overvalue small establishments relative to large ones,
and new establishments relative to producing more at existing ones. Calibrated, the first best raises
consumption 0.81%, mostly by closing marginal establishments. The main result is about firm-size-based
policy. The standard prescription of subsidizing large, high-markup firms removes 98% of intensive-
margin misallocation when establishments are held fixed. Once firms can respond by opening
establishments, the same policy *backfires*: consumption falls 0.76% as large firms over-expand. The
optimal size-dependent policy is non-monotonic. It taxes the smallest and largest firms, subsidizes
large single-establishment firms, and recovers only 6% of the losses. A firm's markup is a poor guide
to how efficient its extensive margin is.

### 3. Market Concentration and Aggregate Productivity: The Role of Demand (Pearce & Wu)
Capitalizes marketing spending of US public firms into a customer-capital stock (marketing averages
4.2% of revenue, versus 2.4% for R&D). Three facts: customer capital tracks revenue more closely than
productivity does (correlation about 0.75 versus 0.50), it is more persistent (AR(1) 0.98 versus 0.86),
and markets are granular (4–7 effective competitors). The model is a dynamic Cournot oligopoly with
two state variables, productivity and customer capital, solved globally with a new method. Because
markups rise with share, the return to marketing peaks for firms that are already ahead. This creates
a "legacy effect": firms keep defending accumulated demand after the productivity behind it fades.
Equalizing the revenue product of customer capital à la Hsieh–Klenow would raise output 52% (41.9
log points). Of that, 44% is due to adjustment costs, 11% to the legacy effect (about 5%, or 4.7 log
points, of aggregate productivity), and 45% is residual. Marketing lowers welfare on net: shutting it
off lowers frontier productivity 4.9% but raises welfare 5.2%. Size-based output subsidies of the
Edmond–Midrigan–Xu type help when small but entrench unproductive incumbents when large.
Concentration can be "good" (driven by productivity) or "bad" (driven by demand) at identical shares
and markups.

### 4. Variable Markups, Incomplete Pass-Throughs, and R&D Misallocation (Adhami, Brouillette, Rockall)
An endogenous-growth model in the Luttmer (2007) style with Kimball (non-isoelastic) demand, so more
productive firms charge higher markups and pass cost savings through incompletely. Firms invest in
process-efficiency R&D, and overhead costs drive selective exit. The new mechanism works through
non-rivalry: an idea's return scales with output, so a markup that shrinks a firm's scale also
depresses its private R&D return. Because markups rise with size, this wedge hits the most
productive firms hardest, and R&D is *misallocated* across firms, not merely too low overall. The
model is estimated by GMM on French administrative firm data (2009–2019), targeting the markup–market-
share gradient and size dispersion; the implied average pass-through matches the data though it is
not targeted. Size-dependent subsidies that remove markup distortions raise long-run TFP growth 1.2pp.
About 75% of that (0.92pp) comes simply from reallocating a *fixed* amount of R&D labor from small to
large firms, with more churn from entry and exit. Correcting only the *level* of markups does little
because the wage bids up R&D costs. Neutralizing markup *dispersion* delivers slightly more than the
full intervention, so dispersion, not the average markup, is what hurts growth.

### 5. Innovation and Pricing Frictions (Liu & Moreira)
Using NielsenIQ product-level scanner data, documents that pricing frictions act at life-cycle,
not business-cycle, horizons. Incumbent products' nominal prices stay nearly flat as they age, so
relative prices fall about 2–3% a year. New products enter at 10–20% premia ("price overshooting"),
and the premia are larger in stickier sectors. Tariff cost shocks do not move incumbent prices but
raise the relative prices of products launched later (a 0.10pp rise in tariff exposure raises the
new-product price by about 0.14pp two years later). The authors embed trend inflation and Calvo
resets tied to product launches in a Klette–Kortum growth model. The estimated friction implies about
9 years to reset to trend, against a 7-year average product life, so most products never re-price.
Identical products therefore carry dispersed markups, which is misallocation. Innovation both suffers
(product values erode) and helps (each new product is a reset option). Banning overshooting cuts
welfare 7% of consumption: −3.8 log points from growth, −4.4 from variety, partly offset by +0.95
from less misallocation. The optimal incumbent R&D subsidy is 41%, far more effective than an entrant
subsidy (19%, which crowds out incumbents), and optimal subsidies rise with pricing frictions.

### 7. Why Don't Old Firms Do New Things? (Crouzet, He, Lyonnet, Yueran Ma)
Asks which new technologies require new firms. The hypothesis is organizational: incumbents struggle
when a technology calls for occupations with different "workstyles" (from O\*NET), because new
business lines must justify themselves against rules built up for the old model, and older firms have
more such rules. Revelio employee reviews confirm that mentions of rules and excessive meetings rise
with firm age. The measure is built in two steps. Patent–occupation task similarity, following Kogan
et al. (2024), predicts each industry's future occupation mix; that mix is mapped into a predicted
five-year change in the industry's employment-weighted workstyle (2003 onward). A one-s.d. larger
predicted workstyle shift raises industry VC investment by about 0.3 log points. Among Compustat
firms, young firms get higher valuations and faster five-year sales and employment growth relative to
old firms when the shift is larger. The same holds across age groups in the Census BDS. The raw
*volume* of new technology does not predict young-firm outperformance, whether measured by all,
breakthrough, or rapidly evolving patents, and the age effect is not a proxy for size or financial
constraints. So organizational frictions, not technology per se, decide whether innovation requires
entry. This recasts the Coasian boundary-of-the-firm question as "which ideas can be implemented
inside a firm?" (Note: this is the Nov 2025 draft; the program abstract adds a
"first-implemented-by-young-firms" result that may be in a later version.)

### 8. Ideas and Firm Dynamics When It Takes Two to Tango (Kim, Olmstead-Rumsey, Wang)
Teams now produce more than 80% of US patents (45% in 1976/80). Revelio/LinkedIn histories linked to
USPTO data show about 90% of co-inventor collaborations happen within the same firm, so firm
boundaries constrain teams. Inventor employment concentration in large firms rose six times more than
overall employment concentration. The model is a Hopenhayn/Melitz-style GE model of the inventor
labor market. Inventors inside a firm draw bilateral match productivities, so team-producing firms
have less-decreasing returns to scale in inventors, which the authors confirm in patent-firm panel
data. Knowledge spillovers across firms depend on size and productivity, following Chikis–Kleinman–
Prato. Comparing two calibrated steady states (1976–99 versus 2000–18), the return-to-scale benefit of
teams rose (plausibly from knowledge specialization). Large firms expand, the inventor wage rises,
small firms shrink or exit, and spillovers fall. Measured against a planner who internalizes
spillovers, the output shortfall grows from 0.2% to 1.7%, which is where the headline "misallocation
amplified six-fold" comes from. The idea-production shift alone accounts for about 73% of that
widening, and spillovers in equilibrium are 6% below the planner's. Implication: subsidies to small
firms and entry are worth more now than before, and "ideas getting harder to find" may partly reflect
inventor reallocation.

### 9. Expectations versus Reality in Business Formation (Dinlersoz & Yueyuan Ma)
Links about 17 million IRS SS-4 EIN applications (2017–2021, the source of Census Business Formation
Statistics) to realized entry and employment in the LBD. Applicants overestimate first-year
employment on average, mainly because many who expect to become employers never do. Those who expect
and achieve entry typically *under*estimate. In Mincer–Zarnowitz tests, realized employment rises far
less than one-for-one with expectations (slope about 0.29, or 0.26 among positive-expectation filers),
rejecting unbiased, efficient forecasts. Forecast efficiency weakened after 2019 with COVID. Errors
vary systematically. Corporations and LLCs are better calibrated than sole proprietors. Places with
more college graduates and denser business activity overestimate less; high-tech and manufacturing
overestimate more. Overestimation also *predicts* higher third-year employment conditional on
first-year outcomes, evidence of private pre-entry information that Jovanovic- and Hopenhayn-style
models lack. The authors rationalize the facts with a Jovanovic learning model with informative
pre-application signals plus heterogeneous, partly uninformative priors. Relative to a common-prior
benchmark, belief noise produces net excess *expected* entry of 8.5–9.5% of applications but net
*forgone actual* employer entry of 0.5–0.9%, and the latter roughly doubled over 2017–2021. Noise
draws weak entrants in and deters some strong ones, which misallocates entrepreneurial talent.

### 10. Innovate to Borrow: How Intangibles Shape Finance and Growth (Asriyan, Martin, Ordoñez, Vanasco)
A Kiyotaki–Moore-style theory (no quantification yet) in which entrepreneurs can pledge assets but
not cash flows and choose between generic technologies, which rely on redeployable physical capital,
and innovative ones, which rely on firm-specific intangibles built through learning or
experimentation. The twist is that intangibles *relax* borrowing constraints despite near-zero
liquidation value. Losing them in default is costly, which raises the continuation value and supports
repayment, giving a microfoundation for cash-flow-based lending. Technology choice is non-monotonic
in productivity. Low types "innovate to experiment" (hoard cash, delay investment), middle types
adopt generic technology under collateral constraints, and high types "innovate to scale" to borrow
more. In general equilibrium, asset prices are the feedback channel. Financial frictions distort both
capital allocation *and* technology choice, producing too little experimentation. The reason is not
poor pledgeability of intangibles but that experimentation relies on reallocating capital to winners.
Directly subsidizing or mandating innovation can backfire by drawing the wrong entrepreneurs into the
wrong kind of innovation. A Pigouvian tax on generic investment improves welfare by lowering asset
prices. Policy message (Europe): the binding problem is scaling winners through capital reallocation,
not seed funding.

### 11. Misallocation due to Incomplete Markets (Baqaee & Burstein)
Treats imperfect risk-sharing and consumption smoothing as resource misallocation. The measure is
Debreu's distance to the frontier: the share of resources left over if markets were completed and
everyone were compensated to their status-quo welfare. It is a multi-agent Lucas (1987) consumption
equivalent that needs no social welfare function or interpersonal comparisons. The authors derive
exact certainty-equivalent formulas and Harberger-triangle approximations that require only
consumption and price data, the EIS, the risk-free rate, and (internationally) a static input-output
table and trade elasticities. No income processes, portfolios, or financial frictions need to be
specified. In a standard Bewley calibration, and directly in PSID household consumption data,
incomplete insurance of idiosyncratic risk destroys about 20% of aggregate consumption in every date
and state. That is about three orders of magnitude above Lucas's cost of business cycles and about
half the veil-of-ignorance gap, which conflates inequality with inefficiency. The authors show that
Benabou-style efficiency measures can prefer more inequality once leisure is endogenous. Across 32
countries and 54 industries (WIOD, 1970–2019), incomplete international markets cost about 5% of world
consumption, but only about 1% excluding China, India, Indonesia, and Korea. The gains come from
domestic insurance and intertemporal trade with fast growers, not from more risk-sharing among rich
countries. Losses rise with a lower EIS and a higher Armington elasticity.
