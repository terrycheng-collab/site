# SITE 2026 — Session 13: Market Failures and Public Policy

**Dates:** Wed Aug 26 2026, 8:30am – Thu Aug 27 2026, 3:40pm PDT
**Location:** Landau Economics Building, 579 Jane Stanford Way, Stanford, CA
**Organizers:** José Ignacio Cuesta (Stanford), Liran Einav (Stanford), Gastón Illanes (Northwestern), Neale Mahoney (Stanford), Pietro Tebaldi (Columbia)
**Source:** https://economics.stanford.edu/events/site-2026/session-13-market-failures-and-public-policy

Theme: IO and public economics on finding market failures and on how well policy fixes them.
The failures covered are (i) market power and price discrimination (target-date funds, pension
funds, brokers, platforms), (ii) asymmetric information and quality problems (health insurance
take-up, nursing-home quality and inspections, hospice), and (iii) externalities and market design
(emissions fraud, battery bidding, innovation rewards). The session then compares the policy tools
on offer: enforcement, public options, price caps, subsidy design, inspection timing, vouchers,
contracts and antitrust remedies.

## Papers read — summaries

### 1. Fraud, Evasion, and Enforcement in Decentralized Environmental Regulation (Bradt & Dorsey)
Texas hands emissions testing to about 5,500 private stations, and that creates a market for
"clean-scan" fraud. The paper uses 100M inspection records (2016–2024) and a new detection
method. It combines several fraud indicators that come from independent data streams, so only a
shared latent cause (fraud) can make them move together. A mixture model then recovers
station-level fraud rates from that covariance. About 11.2% of inspections are fraudulent (≈10.7M
tests), peaking at 13.4% in 2022 before the "Operation Cinderblock" crackdown. Roadside remote
sensing shows that fraud-passed cars emit more than three times the NO and HC of legitimate
passes, about $121 per car per year in health damages and $1.1bn cumulatively. A nested-logit
demand model uses the fact that repair costs vary with which component failed. At $93 repairs only
2% choose noncompliance, rising to 24% at $276 and 68% at $750. The median switch point is $501.
Counterfactual: shutting down every fraud station takes fraud from 13.6% to 0, but repair rises only
3.8pp. Most displaced drivers "abandon" and many drive unregistered instead. The authors call this
**enforcement leakage**. Pollution benefits roughly offset or beat drivers' surplus losses, but the
rents corrupt stations earn exceed the environmental gains. That explains why fraud persists, and it
suggests pairing station closures with demand-side tools (on-road registration enforcement, repair
subsidies).

### 2. Battery Bidding and Market Efficiency in Energy Transition (Chen, Allcott, Park)
Uses ERCOT 15-minute bidding and settlement data for every utility-scale battery (2018–2025),
marked "preliminary". Batteries bid actively: they post high discharge offers to keep the option
value of stored energy, cut offers sharply as prices near their peak, react quickly to price shocks,
and rely on very recent prices to forecast. The authors build a dynamic bidding model inside a
market equilibrium, with battery cycling costs estimated by SMM at ≈$4–20/MWh. The model shows the
cost of **bid lead time**, the rule in CAISO, MISO, SPP and ISO-NE that bids be committed ahead of
dispatch. A 30-minute lead time cuts battery profits ≈17% and a 75-minute lead time ≈25%, because
batteries discharge too early and miss the peaks. The losses are larger with more volatile prices
and smaller for longer-duration batteries. Letting bids depend on state of charge recovers 12–16%
of profit. Removing lead time lowers prices, especially in the upper tail. The gains go to consumers
and batteries at conventional generators' expense, and batteries complement solar more. Takeaway: a
dull market-design timing rule has first-order efficiency costs for storage.

### 3. Default Options and Market Power: Evidence from Target-Date Funds (Loseto & Yang)
Target-date funds, the dominant 401(k) default with ≈$4tn in assets, are funds-of-funds. That lets
managers sell the same underlying funds twice: directly to fee-sensitive investors and, wrapped, to
fee-insensitive default investors. The "wrapper fee" averaged 22bp in 2019 (≈60bp at the 90th
percentile). That is more than 100% of the underlying fees and costs investors over $3bn a year. The
paper argues it is not explained by rebalancing costs (the TSP's TDFs charge about the underlying
fees) or by cross-subsidies to recordkeeping. TDF flows are much less fee-sensitive. A BLP-style
model of employers choosing defaults, participants opting in or out, and non-TDF investors, with
Hausman and cost-shifter instruments, gives TDF elasticities below 1 against 2–3 for non-TDF
investors. Implied TDF markups are ≈20bp higher, worth ≈$2.8bn of profit. Counterfactuals: banning
the wrapper fee (no price discrimination) cuts TDF fees 13bp and raises TDF investors' welfare by
$1.2bn/yr, while managers raise non-TDF fees only 3bp and lose 1.3% of profit. A 20bp fee cap is
worth +$1.9bn. Making employers as fee-sensitive as outside investors cuts TDF fees 27bp. Defaults
fix inertia on saving but create market power on price.

### 4. Regulation by Public Options: Evidence from Pension Funds (Blanchard, Fleitas, Gonzalez Valdenegro)
Uruguay's individual-account pension system has one high-quality state-owned administrator (SOE)
competing with three private ones, and workers are inert. The paper uses administrative data on
workers' enrollment (1996–2020) and a dynamic model. Workers pick a fund at entry, then re-optimize
only after an "awareness shock". Forward-looking firms set fees and returns, trading off investing
in new cohorts against harvesting existing ones. The SOE's objective adds weight on workers' savings.
Estimation recovers positive enrollment marginal costs (sales-force payments), unlike earlier work
that assumed zero. Privatizing the SOE would raise average fees 44%, the ex-SOE's own fee by more
than 150%, and private rivals' fees ≈8%. Returns fall 3%, and the representative worker's retirement
savings fall 6.7% (11% for ex-SOE enrollees). Making workers more fee-responsive helps but cannot
realistically offset privatization. Making the SOE even more non-profit-minded backfires on
low-wage workers left at private funds, because segmentation lets private funds harvest inert
stayers. The 2020 fee cap does better: it raises savings for everyone, especially low earners.
Bottom line: direct price regulation beats a public option as the competition tool here.

### 9. Dying Better: Hospice Care and the End of Life (Ji, Kong, Roy)
The design is a stacked difference-in-differences around 1,518 county hospice-agency entries
(1999–2019). It links Medicare claims to nursing-home Minimum Data Set clinical assessments, which
lets the authors measure pain, function and care processes, not just spending. Entry raises hospice
spending $4.26 per beneficiary-quarter (+7.1%) and cuts non-hospice spending $9.00, mostly
inpatient (−$5.29) and SNF (−$2.12). That is a **≈2:1 fiscal return**. Care becomes less intensive
and more comfort-oriented (fewer catheters and restraints, more swallowing therapy and sleep
medication). Deaths shift from institutions to home, with no harm to mortality, pain or function.
Take-up and offsets vary widely by diagnosis. For dementia hospice roughly breaks even, while for
COPD and heart disease it saves ≈$3 per $1. A referral model decomposes this: providers refer at an
average quarterly mortality cutoff of 11.7% (6.7–17.4% across groups), and the cutoff is higher
where referral costs them more revenue (avg. $36k forgone). Capacity and access constraints add to
this. Removing financial disincentives and expanding capacity could raise patient welfare up to
64%. Hospice is underused because of provider incentives and supply barriers such as
certificate-of-need laws and workforce shortages.

### 10. Predictably Unpredictable Inspections (Gandhi, Olenski, Shi)
Nursing-home inspections are "unannounced" but roughly annual, so the hazard of an inspection is
near zero for about 35 weeks and then climbs steeply. The same pattern holds for restaurant, mine,
HUD and FDA inspections. Using daily payroll staffing and assessment-based care inputs, the authors
show facilities slack right after an inspection and ramp up effort as the next one nears. Patient
mortality mirrors this, and a one-SD rise in effort lowers mortality 5.4%. Because effort is
*concave* in inspection risk, spreading risk evenly raises average effort. A dynamic model captures
both the incentive and information roles of inspections. The current regime saves 886 lives a year
compared with no inspections and cuts regulators' uncertainty about quality by 56%. Making the
timing fully unpredictable, at the same frequency, saves **10% more lives**, the same gain as 10%
more inspections, at almost no budget cost. It loses only 2.7% of information. Unpredictability
also makes extra frequency 50% more effective. The timing of inspections is an underused lever.

### 11. Government Monitoring of Health Care Quality: Evidence from the Nursing Home Sector (Chen & Dillender)
The companion paper to #10, focused on behavior during the inspection window itself. Daily
PBJ staffing and MDS data within nursing-home × time-category cells show sharp **window-dressing**
while inspectors are on site. On the peak day nurse hours rise 10.2 and aide hours 6.6, with
increases in every staff type, and the skill mix shifts toward RNs. Admissions fall 9% and
temporary discharges (mostly parked in hospitals, then readmitted) rise 4%, both of which raise
staff-to-patient ratios. Vaccinations go up. Nearly all of this vanishes the day after the
inspection ends. Responses are larger at for-profits, at facilities with low prior ratings, and in
competitive or high-staff-availability markets. Ratings still carry information. A
McClellan-style differential-distance IV (5.6M admissions) shows that entering a
higher-rated home lowers 90-day mortality 0.8pp (−5.2%), and the effect persists for at least a
year. It is stronger where gaming is weaker. Ratings explain ≈10% of the variance in facility
mortality effects. Citations with precise criteria and larger fines produce lasting quality gains.
So inspections mis-measure *absolute* quality but rank *relative* quality.

### 12. Patent Vouchers as Innovation Incentives (Dubois, Moisson, Tirole) — *read from the Dec 2022 earlier version, "The Economics of Transferable Patent Extensions"*
The question is how to reward antibiotic and neglected-disease innovators when sales cannot pay for
the innovation. Instead of a cash prize, the inventor gets a transferable exclusivity extension
(TEE, a "voucher") and sells it to a pharma firm that uses it to extend exclusivity on another drug.
The key statistic is the **cost-over-reward ratio**: social surplus lost per $1 delivered to the
inventor, compared with 1+λ (the marginal cost of public funds) for a cash prize. The length of
the extension does not affect this ratio. Theory: when generic competition after the patent ends is
*imperfect* (captive brand consumers, regulated generic prices such as France's 60% rule), extending
exclusivity destroys little consumer surplus. It mainly moves rents from generics makers, while the
voucher sale raises revenue. Empirics use all drugs sold in 15 European countries, 2002–2012. In
most countries a $1 voucher reward costs consumers and taxpayers less than $1, so less than a cash
prize. In an EU-wide union, 10 countries would clearly prefer vouchers, 3 are nearly indifferent
and 2 prefer cash. The paper also covers adverse selection in which drugs buy vouchers, voucher
auctions, and scalability: rents to inframarginal buyers grow with the number of vouchers.
*(The 2026 conference version may have updated data and results.)*

### 13. Generous Long-Term Contracts (Chassang)
Theory with calibration. Consumers who judge a menu of contracts adversarially (they are ambiguity
averse) will not adopt a complex or high-powered contract unless it dominates the default. EDF's
"Tempo" variable-price electricity plan saves nearly all households ≈20% but only ≈3% adopt it.
Chassang's fix is a single "generous" contract: at every date, cumulative payments equal those under
whichever menu option would have been cheapest *in hindsight*. It dominates the default, so
skeptical consumers adopt it. The surprise is that when preferences and states are i.i.d. (or
learning is fast), the generous contract approximately implements the rational-consumer outcome of
the original menu. Under slow learning the result extends to "Pareto alignments": contracts that mix
the default with the producer's profit change. They deliver an approximate Pareto improvement at a
per-period profit penalty of order 1/√N. Calibrating to French electricity load shedding, the
penalty is over 10% of profit with a pooled counterfactual-profit benchmark. It falls to ≈5% at
c=0 and below 1% at c=10% with individualized benchmarks that absorb persistent household
differences. There is a parallel calibration for health-insurance cost sharing. Main lesson: firms
may not need to offer a menu at all.

### 14. Gatekeepers and Self-Preferencing (Decarolis & Li)
The model has a dominant platform that sells a monopolized primary product (publisher ad server)
and a competitively supplied ancillary service (ad exchange/SSP). Sellers choose and pay for the
ancillary service, but buyers (advertisers) also care which one is used. Self-preferencing means
degrading outcomes for sellers who use rival services. It can be profitable even when the
gatekeeper's service is *lower* quality, which contradicts the Chicago single-monopoly-profit
logic, and network effects strengthen the incentive. Its welfare effect depends on the sign. If the
platform charges only sellers, sellers may gain (the platform cuts the primary price to attract
them) while buyers lose if they prefer rivals. With two-sided pricing either side can lose. Applied
to the French Competition Authority's 2021 Google adtech remedy (sharing auction data with rival
SSPs, publisher side only), synthetic-control evidence shows publisher revenue and advertiser
counts *fell* after June 2022. Estimates show French advertisers value Google-adtech publishers much
more, so pushing publishers to rivals shrank participation on both sides. A modest remedy on the
advertiser side would have offset the loss. Structural separation beats behavioral remedies only if
it raises rival quality for *both* sides. There is no one-size remedy, and DMA-style rules must be
two-sided.
