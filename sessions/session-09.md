## Papers read — summaries

### 1. Generous Long-Term Contracts (Chassang)
Why do consumers shun efficient but risky-looking contracts? EDF's variable-price "Tempo" electricity contract, for example, gets about 3% take-up even though nearly everyone would save. Chassang proposes replacing the menu with a single **generous long-term contract**. At every horizon, the consumer's cumulative payments equal what they would have paid under the contract that turns out best for them in hindsight, so even an ambiguity-averse consumer adopts it. Because errors average out over a long relationship, this guarantee costs the producer almost nothing: in conditionally i.i.d. or fast-learning environments, profit converges to what the menu earns from a rational chooser (Props 1–2). With slow learning, "Pareto-aligned" contracts still come within a penalty of order 1/√N (Prop 4). A calibration on 10,000 simulated French households gives a key design lesson. Counterfactual-profit benchmarks must be individualized, because persistent household heterogeneity explains most consumption variation (R² 0.10 pooled vs. 0.67 individual). A temperature-only benchmark leaves a penalty above 10% of profits, while an individualized one pushes it below 1%. A parallel health-insurance application offers a generous version of an alternative to low-deductible PPOs, which 85% of employees pick.

### 2. Duality and Recursion in Dynamic Games (Rahman)
A methods paper that rebuilds dynamic programming for repeated games with imperfect monitoring around duality, with welfare weights rather than continuation values as the state. For two equilibrium notions, ppRE (public randomization) and ppCE (a mediator making secret recommendations), extremal welfare is an LP sequence problem with no duality gap (Thms 3.1–3.2). Its recursive version is a convex, non-contraction functional equation whose maximal fixed point is the value (Thm 4.1). The dual solution exists in weighted-ℓ¹ (Thm 4.3) and a convergent algorithm computes it (Thm 6.1). Applications: in a repeated Prisoners' Dilemma where pairwise identifiability fails, **no ppRE can sustain mutual cooperation** (payoff sum ≤ 1), but ppCE mediation can. In dynamic contracting, welfare weights follow a martingale that generalizes Rogerson's inverse Euler equation, and optimal lotteries show up near retirement boundaries. With costly monitoring, the principal monitors to avoid costly firing and can monitor secretly (hence rarely) under ppCE. Duality also shows welfare is Lipschitz in IC errors (a robustness result for approximate equilibrium). In the frequent-action limit it yields an HJB "welfare optimality" equation linking the curvature of welfare to welfare itself.

### 3. Long-Term Contracts, Commitment, and Optimal Information Disclosure (Dovis & Martellini)
A two-period insurance/lending model in which the incumbent firm privately learns a consumer's persistent income type, and a regulator picks a public disclosure policy. **With commitment, disclose nothing**, since disclosure raises high types' outside option and squeezes cross-subsidies to low types. **Without commitment**, low types never get cross-subsidized under any policy, because outsiders know that anyone they poach is a low type. Partial disclosure becomes optimal once the share of high types exceeds a cutoff. It takes a **bad-news** form: all high types and some low types get "good," which equalizes high types' consumption over time through front-loaded, rebated incumbent profits. Long-term relationships can **hurt** consumers when high types dominate, because spot markets allow cross-subsidies. With productive outsiders (fintechs), full disclosure is still never optimal. Mapped to lending: loan rates rise over the course of a relationship (consistent with Ioannidou–Ongena), and switchers are adversely selected (consistent with Di Maggio–Yao). Unrestricted open banking or data ownership, even opt-in, is never optimal. Coarse credit ratings that pool high and some low types, or a right to data erasure, can be.

### 4. Dilutive Financing (Ryu)
A new theory of lumpy and early firm financing driven by financier bargaining power, with no fixed transaction costs. If a negotiation fails, the firm reaches an alternative financier only at rate γ. The bargaining surplus therefore includes the value of avoiding delay, so every financing round dilutes firm value. Firms respond with lumpy (s,S) financing to bargain less often (Prop 1), and they finance *early*, while holding slack, to bargain from a stronger outside option. Rents therefore depend on strategy (Lemma 3). Firms that wait until funds run out pay fixed-cost-like rents (1−θ)(V − h̄). Firms that finance early pay variable-cost-like rents (φ/γ)·Δh, which **do not depend on bargaining power**, so small observed costs can hide powerful financiers. Firms with better outside financing options keep *more* slack (Prop 3), the reverse of search-friction models. In an investment extension calibrated to Bolton–Chen–Wang, investment irreversibility pushes unproductive firms, which cannot cut spending to improve their position, into late, expensive financing. Only productive firms finance early, so irreversibility widens cross-sectional differences in financing costs.

### 5. Frequent Stress Tests (Basak, Choudhary & Zhou)
Motivated by SVB's failure before its next scheduled test, the paper models stress tests as dynamic information design in an asynchronous bank-run global game. Creditors split into N sequential groups, each seeing the results of all tests so far plus a private signal. A policy (N, k) is **credible** if the worst equilibrium has no bank failing after passing. Thm 1 characterizes credibility by a single condition on the first cohort, proved via a "widening gap" property. Thm 2 shows **frequency and stringency are substitutes**: the minimum credible stringency k(N) is decreasing and convex in N, because frequent tests limit how much damage (x − k)/N a skeptical first cohort can do before the next reassurance. Credibility is harder when creditors are keen to run or have precise signals. With convex testing costs, the optimal policy is a finite frequency with the least stringent credible test, and it tests more often (and more leniently) when shocks are likely or systemic externalities are large. Extensions: banks prefer fewer tests than the planner and can impose that choice through their staggered debt-maturity structure, while tighter LCR liquidity rules raise the optimal frequency.

### 6. The Limits of Consumption Taxation (Berriel & Jagadeesan)
The classic case against capital taxation assumes consumption is observable, but owner-managers disguise consumption as business expenses (31% of household expenses in Portuguese data). Using continuous-time dynamic mechanism design with Epstein–Zin capitalists who face unverifiable idiosyncratic risk (in the style of DeMarzo–Sannikov and Di Tella), the authors solve the long-run optimum in closed form. With a sufficiently patient planner, the steady state is unique and saddle-path stable. It has a **positive long-run savings wedge** 2(ρ−ζ)/(ψ−1), capital below the modified golden rule, and capitalist consumption proportional to how easily they can disguise it. Constant linear taxes on consumption, unexpected capital income and wealth implement it. They keep capitalists indifferent between consuming openly and consuming through the firm. Easy disguise means lower consumption taxes and a positive wealth tax, while hard disguise means high consumption taxes and a wealth subsidy. The illustrative calibration gives a 30–60% capital-income tax (42.85% at ζ = 2%). If total business expenses are verifiable, the planner also caps deductions.

### 7. Social Learning with Correlated Information (Awaya & Krishna)
This is the classic binary herding model (Banerjee; Bikhchandani-Hirshleifer-Welch), except that agents' private signals follow a Markov chain, so each signal is copied from the previous agent with some probability. The motivation is information monoculture: reposts, AI models trained on the same data, "churnalism." There is an essentially unique equilibrium, public beliefs form a finite Markov chain, and a herd always forms. The probability that the herd is wrong has a closed form. It rises smoothly with persistence but jumps *down* countably infinitely many times, and its total variation is unbounded. The reason is that more persistent signals make predecessors' actions less informative, so it takes more identical actions to override one's own signal. That delays herding and lets more information through. Some persistence can even beat independent signals. More similar signals can make actions *less* similar, and with enough persistence almost any finite action history, including apparent contrarianism, is consistent with equilibrium. The upshot is that worries that common information sources cause correlated mistakes (for example, banks holding the same portfolios) do not follow from theory in general.

### 8. Learning and the Emergence of Nonlinearity in Financial Markets (Dew-Becker, Giglio & Molavi)
A general filtering model: the price is the posterior mean of a latent fundamental that can follow any process, including disasters. A new cumulant recursion says each posterior cumulant's sensitivity to news equals the next cumulant times signal precision. This generates, in one framework, crashes and left skew, volatility tied to uncertainty, long memory (fast then slow mean reversion), and a leverage effect. The leverage effect exists *iff* beliefs are negatively skewed, and its strength is κ3/(σY κ2). A calibration with Barro-Jin disaster jumps and random-walk fundamentals matches the data: return–volatility correlation −0.76 in the model vs −0.78 in the data, plus skewness by horizon and volatility autocorrelations. Daily regressions support the model's volatility law of motion. The coefficient on scaled skew×return is 1.01 (s.e. 0.03) for the S&P 500 and 1.13 for natural gas, and quadratic mean reversion dominates linear. Model-free estimates put investors' posterior s.d. of fundamentals at 10.4–16.5%, close to the 17% disagreement in the Yale investor survey. Believing that crashes are possible is enough to produce the rich higher-order dynamics.

### 9. Reputational Spillovers (Kuvalekar & Sanktjohanser)
A center bargains at the same time with two peripherals in Abreu-Gul wars of attrition, and the center has one global commitment type, so a concession in one dispute reveals flexibility in both. The game has a unique equilibrium: a possible time-0 atom, then (if a peripheral is uniquely the most reputable) a phase where that peripheral waits while the weaker one concedes *faster* than in isolation, then everyone concedes at the bilateral rate until a common terminal time. Spillovers affect payoffs *iff* a peripheral starts as uniquely most reputable. In that case the strongest peripheral loses, the weakest gains, and when the center's reputation lies between the two, the center is strictly worse off than in two separate negotiations. The gap survives as commitment probabilities go to zero. The weak peripheral benefits most when the center's bigger fight is elsewhere, which fits holdout creditors in sovereign restructurings and small unions. Sequential bargaining and partial observability make the center's disadvantage worse, and a four-player star behaves the same way.

### 10. Dynamic Disclosure with(out) Timestamps (Kolb & Zhou)
The September 2026 draft. A sender gets one piece of hard evidence about a Markov-switching binary state at a random time and chooses when to disclose it. With timestamps there is a unique equilibrium: good news is disclosed immediately, and bad news after a deterministic delay tied to its date, because proving the evidence is old softens the belief drop. This equilibrium does not depend on the discount rate and satisfies the Minimum Principle. Without timestamps and with a high prior, a stock of undisclosed good evidence always builds up, since senders delay to make old good news look fresh. With an absorbing bad state there is a unique three-phase equilibrium of stockpiling, stochastic purging, and transparency, and the stock must run down continuously, not all at once. With an impatient sender, bad evidence comes out in isolated bursts whose spacing grows without bound as impatience rises. The sender is indifferent ex ante, but receivers are better off with timestamps (Blackwell dominance over the three-phase equilibrium). Mandating dates on evidence speeds up good news and makes bad-news disclosure possible, which also explains sales sandbagging and recycled "fresh" news.

### 11. Best Garbling is No Garbling: Persuasion in Real Time (Urgun & Whitmeyer)
A continuous-time Brownian persuasion problem. The sender can garble the evidence stream (noise, silence, batching, marked messages) and choose when the receiver must act, and pays a convex delay cost. Full transparency reaches any target distribution of posteriors in the least expected time, and all transparent rules that do so have the same mean duration. Among them, Root's barrier embedding minimizes dispersion in convex order. The main theorem therefore says transparency plus the right stopping rule weakly beats every clock-dominated garbling policy, strictly under strict convexity: all persuasion should happen through *when* to stop. When the receiver chooses when to stop, any equilibrium with an "unbanked" disclosure clock has a transparent acceleration that makes both players weakly better off, with the receiver gaining exactly her saved waiting cost. Higher delay costs lower persuasion probabilities, and a higher signal-to-noise ratio is a pure time change that raises them. This is a clean result for rolling reviews, trials, and underwriting.

### 12. Sequential Sampling under Adversarial Manipulation (Brandl & Shi)
Alice samples a bad-news Poisson process to learn a binary state. Bob, who wants the high action and is just as uninformed, can secretly and adaptively suppress breakdowns at a linear cost. In every equilibrium Alice stops at a deterministic time, and Bob mixes between never manipulating and manipulating at full intensity from s1 to her exit time. Manipulation equilibria exist for priors above γ/λ and share the same s1 and t1, so they differ only in Bob's mixing probability. They coexist with a no-manipulation Wald equilibrium that Alice strictly prefers and Bob strictly dislikes. Because of this, Wald's classic ranking can flip: committing to a fixed sample deters manipulation and can beat the worst sequential equilibrium, and a hybrid protocol (sequential exploration, then a committed confirmatory phase) can beat both. Symmetric uncertainty and adaptive manipulation are essential. An informed Bob forces immediate exit, and up-front, non-adaptive manipulation brings back the Wald outcome. The paper is a theoretical case for pre-registered confirmatory phases in trials, audits, and A/B tests.

### 13. Time Trumps Quantity in the Market for Lemons (Fuchs, Gottardi, Moreira)
Read from the January 2024 AEA/ASSA version. The paper puts dynamic adverse selection (Akerlof, Fuchs-Skrzypacz) and quantity signaling (Leland-Pyle, DeMarzo-Duffie) in one model: a privately informed seller of a divisible asset trades over time with competitive buyers. With long-term commitment, only the discounted quantity Q(c) is pinned down, so delay and partial sales are interchangeable, and welfare does not depend on trading frequency (unlike the indivisible case). With spot contracts and public trade histories, the equilibrium is unique, each type sells out within two periods, and each gets its full commitment payoff. In the continuous-time limit each type sells *everything* at one date τ(c) with e^(−rτ) = Q(c), so only delay signals quality. The reason is that time is irreversible and acts as built-in commitment, while a promise to retain part of the asset is never credible. With private trade histories and discrete dates the equivalence breaks down: residual supply becomes a second dimension of private information, a middle tier of types pools, and welfare cannot be ranked against the public regime. The equivalence returns under continuous trading. If supply rises with quality, trade is immediate and first-best. The main practical point is that trade transparency and trading frequency interact in market design.

### 14. Automation, AI, and the Intergenerational Transmission of Knowledge (Ide)
An OLG task-based growth model where novices learn tacit, non-verifiable, non-pledgeable skills by working with experts. The wage zero lower bound means novices can pay for mentorship only with labor, which gives an "oversubscribed" top tier of rationed junior positions. With fat-tailed skills, growth is (N−I)^θ, driven by top experts' span of control. Expanding automation raises output on impact but permanently lowers growth, so output eventually falls below the no-shock path. This happens even with no fall in entry-level employment, because novices are reallocated to worse mentors. Laissez-faire adoption lowers welfare whenever the discount factor exceeds a threshold, because of the incomplete knowledge-transfer contract. Scale effects (more supervision capacity for top experts) can reverse this, but the paper reads early generative-AI evidence as displacement. New labor-intensive tasks unambiguously raise growth and welfare. Policy options include limits or taxes on entry-level automation, subsidies for mentorship-rich junior jobs, and residency-style training. The statistic to track is *who* juniors work for, not how many are hired.

### 15. Employer Competition and Certification (Chopra)
A monopoly certifier sells menus of tests to an applicant with private information about his ability, and must also persuade two vertically differentiated employers who commit to hiring standards. Second-degree price discrimination causes two distortions relative to a certifier that observes types. It *excludes* low types, raising unemployment, and it *coarsens* information by steering pooled applicants toward the less selective employer, which cuts information rents (Theorem 1: with s_t > s_b, some high-ability applicants are sent to the bottom employer). In equilibrium this weakens the top employer's incentive to screen aggressively. For a range of parameters the top employer never sets the maximal standard, although the benchmark requires it (Theorem 2), so standards converge and even a weak bottom employer gains market power. Extensions: with wages, the certifier pools until employers break even, so differentiation alone gives no wage competition. Charging employers platform fees can make tests more informative. The lesson is that regulating certification markets (licensing, rating agencies) affects downstream competition in non-obvious ways.

### 16. What You Can't See: Contingent Thinking Failures in Dynamic Markets (Cohen)
The paper defines Dynamic Cursed Expectations: agents learn correctly from observed history and know each future variable's correct marginal distribution, but treat different future periods as independent, so they do not anticipate learning from current or future prices. It also defines the market equilibrium DCEE, proves it exists for CARA economies with riskless storage and next-period private signals, and applies it to a multi-asset mean-variance model. Failing to learn from the current price produces trade where REE has none, with closed-form volume that rises with the number of signals. Failing to anticipate the resale-price/dividend correlation leads traders to underestimate variance, so risk aversion becomes excess risk-taking. The price bias versus REE shrinks over time and is negative at the final date, but assets are overvalued at all earlier dates whenever dividend variance is at least signal noise, with larger bias for riskier assets and longer horizons. A partially cursed version scales the bias linearly by χ. The paper motivates the concept with lab evidence (limit-order vs. market-order learning gaps) and suggests uses in mortgages, investment, and inflation forecasting.

### 17. Implicit Incentive Provision with Misspecified Learning (Echenique & Li)
A career-concerns model where an evaluator chooses how intensively to assess an agent (reducing noise), and society holds a dogmatic, wrong belief about the agent's ability while learning about effort productivity (Berk-Nash equilibrium, selected by stability with stochastic-approximation learning foundations). If ability is underestimated, surprisingly good outcomes are attributed to higher productivity, assessment and effort rise, and there is a unique, stable, small-distortion equilibrium. If ability is overestimated, pessimism about productivity lowers assessment, which deepens pessimism. There can be multiple equilibria and cascades to extreme beliefs even from small errors. For identical groups, the underrated group is scrutinized more and works harder, but society overstates the effort gap even more, so the ability stereotype spreads to effort. Reward gaps can narrow or reverse when markets reward effort, which can look like equity while the bias is entrenched. Intensifying assessment (pay-for-performance, better testing, curriculum alignment) reduces distortions, and color-sighted affirmative action stops the downward belief cascade.

### 18. Strategic Learning with Asymmetric Rationality (Liu & Miao)
A fully rational biased sender controls whether unfalsifiable i.i.d. signals keep arriving. The receiver is an optimally designed finite automaton with m memory states and no discounting. The optimal protocols are "parsimonious": two absorbing decision states, with every transient state recommending the sender-unfavorable action. Stopping early therefore guarantees the sender's worst outcome, and she behaves as if non-strategic. The receiver's optimal payoff equals the Hellman-Cover payoff with m−1 states against a non-strategic sender, so manipulation costs exactly one memory state. Classic non-strategic optimal automata are fully manipulable. The sender prefers more informative signals when the prior is against her and less when it favors her. Near-optimal protocols absorb only from the penultimate state on extreme signals and with vanishing probability. This produces hesitation near the decision point, disengagement from information once a decision is reached, and polarization from identical evidence, all as equilibrium features rather than psychological assumptions. Applications include FDA and SEC review and voters facing strategic media.
