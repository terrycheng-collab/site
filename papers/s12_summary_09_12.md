# The Consumer Welfare Effects of Bank Mergers
**Ariel Pakes, Michael D. Whinston, Fanyin Zheng (October 2024)**

**Question.** Bank merger review has traditionally focused on price effects (deposit and loan rates). This paper asks whether *non-price* effects — especially branch closures — are a quantitatively important, and possibly dominant, channel through which mergers affect consumer welfare.

**Data/methods.** The authors combine FDIC Summary of Deposits (branch-level deposits, locations, openings/closings, 2000–2018), RateWatch branch-level deposit rates, FDIC Call Report small-business loan and asset data, SNL/S&P bank M&A records (2011–2018), and Census (ACS/CBP) and Survey of Consumer/Small Business Finances data. They study 55 U.S. counties that experienced exactly one bank merger in 2015 with geographic branch overlap, comparing them to 2,565 no-merger counties. Because branch-level deposit data don't separate consumer and business deposits, they build a structural discrete-choice model (BLP-style) of (i) small-business banking-relationship formation (loan- and deposit-side) and (ii) consumer deposit supply across nearby branches, with banks setting deposit rates optimally, identified via cost-side instruments for rival banks' rate-setting regions.

**Findings.** (1) Distance to a branch matters enormously: a 1% closer branch raises small-business relationship formation by ~4.1% and consumer deposits by ~2.4%, equivalent for a median consumer to a 1.6% rise in deposit rates; the estimated consumer deposit-rate elasticity is ~1.6. (2) Post-merger, merged banks cut branch growth by a statistically significant 18% and deposit growth by 7%, while deposit-rate changes were small and insignificant once controls are added. (3) Overall consumer welfare fell 8.9% in single-merger counties vs. 0.2% in no-merger counties — an 8.7 percentage-point gap, equivalent to a 35% cut in deposit rates. (4) Decomposing the gap: branch-network changes account for a 4.5 pp welfare loss (statistically significant, roughly twice the size of the rate-driven effect of 2.8 pp), while quality changes are negative but insignificant.

**Why it matters.** The paper shows antitrust merger review that evaluates only price effects would miss the largest and most robust channel of consumer harm — branch closures — with direct implications for bank merger policy and local retail-banking competition.

**Deposit-franchise link.** The results imply that branch proximity, not just the deposit rate, is a core determinant of deposit-franchise value — consolidation that erodes branch density destroys "stickiness"-related consumer surplus even when rates barely move.

# Interest Rate Risk and Cross-Sectional Effects of Micro-Prudential Regulation
**Juliane Begenau, Vadim Elenev, Tim Landvoigt (April 2025)**

**Question.** How do banks' joint choices of interest-rate risk (securities portfolios) and funding structure (insured vs. uninsured deposits) generate cross-sectional financial fragility, and which micro-prudential tools best mitigate run risk without excessive collateral damage to lending and liquidity provision?

**Data/methods.** Using FFIEC 031/041 call-report panel data (2010Q1–2022Q4), the authors document three facts: banking is highly concentrated (top 1% of banks hold over half of assets); uninsured deposit share rises with bank size (top 10% of banks issue 90% of uninsured deposits); and the securities/cash share is U-shaped in bank size. They build a two-period general-equilibrium model of heterogeneous banks that choose a portfolio of credit-risky loans and interest-rate-risky bonds, and a funding mix of insured/differentiated uninsured deposits, with endogenous depositor runs (à la Dávila-Goldstein) and asymmetric CES-based market power across deposit types. The model is calibrated jointly to loan/asset concentration, bond-share targets by size, and deposit-rate/uninsured-share/Gini moments.

**Findings.** (1) The model reproduces the empirical U-shaped bond share and rising uninsured-deposit concentration purely from differential market power (more differentiated insured vs. uninsured deposit markets) and productivity heterogeneity — not from too-big-to-fail (TBTF) guarantees, though adding size-dependent bailout probabilities further amplifies large-bank risk-taking. (2) In the baseline, small banks fail mainly from solvency shocks (up to ~1.5% probability) while large banks' failures are run-driven, reaching just over 5% in bad states; an unanticipated rate-hike shock roughly doubles large-bank run-default probability (toward ~10%) while raising small-bank default rates by only about 10 bps. (3) When banks rationally anticipate higher symmetric bond-return volatility, large banks respond by holding fewer precautionary bonds, which paradoxically *increases* fragility — rational anticipation of rate risk does not make portfolios safer. (4) Among policy tools: an unconditional tighter capital requirement (θ, ~3 pp) nearly eliminates run defaults among the top 0.1% of banks but reduces liquidity provision and loans economy-wide; a liquidity requirement tied to uninsured deposits reduces run risk with less than half the effectiveness of size-dependent capital rules and distorts lending allocation (raising dispersion in marginal product of capital); a size-dependent capital requirement matches the unconditional policy's run-risk reduction while remaining close to welfare-neutral, since it leaves smaller banks' leverage undisturbed.

**Why it matters.** The paper provides a quantitative laboratory connecting bank size, deposit-market power, and interest-rate exposure to run risk, showing that "one-size-fits-all" regulation (uniform capital or liquidity rules) trades off run-risk reduction against lending/liquidity provision, whereas size-contingent capital requirements target the actual locus of fragility (large, uninsured-deposit-funded banks) with minimal side effects — directly informing the post-2023 debate on regulating large regional/systemic banks' interest-rate risk.

**Deposit-franchise link.** The model's core mechanism — banks' willingness to hold rate-sensitive bonds to protect a profitable but market-power-derived deposit franchise — ties deposit franchise value directly to interest rate risk-taking, and shows that eroding this franchise's insurance role (via anticipated bond risk or TBTF guarantees) actively increases large-bank run fragility.
