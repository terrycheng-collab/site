# The Declining Role of Deposits in Credit Creation
**Naz Koont, Stefan Walz (July 2026)**

**Question.** Beyond banks' direct lending response to a deposit inflow, credit itself generates new deposits as loan proceeds circulate and partly resettle as stable bank funding — an amplification loop dating back to Tobin (1963) that has received little empirical or structural attention. How large is this credit-deposit feedback loop, how has it evolved, and what does it imply for the *total* effect of a deposit inflow on bank credit creation?

**Data/methods.** The authors first estimate a reduced-form "credit-deposit multiplier" using weekly U.S. banking-system data (2005–2019), instrumenting deposit inflows with high-frequency variation in the timing of U.S. Treasury cash settlements and tracing the cumulative deposit response over subsequent weeks. Because this reduced-form multiplier conflates two margins — banks' direct credit response to a marginal deposit dollar, and the "recycling rate" (the fraction of resulting credit proceeds that return as stable deposits) — they then build a structural two-layer equilibrium model (a static portfolio-allocation layer disciplining banks' direct credit response via balance-sheet/capital/liquidity costs and non-bank competition, plus a monetary-creation layer summarized by the recycling rate) and estimate its parameters by deterministic minimum distance, jointly fitting banking-sector pricing conditions and the early- vs. late-period reduced-form multipliers.

**Findings.** (1) The reduced-form credit-deposit multiplier is sizable but has weakened sharply: a $1 deposit inflow raises cumulative deposits by $2.06 on average, but the multiplier fell from $2.38 (first half of sample) to $1.36 (second half). (2) The structural decomposition shows both underlying margins declined: banks' direct credit response fell from 0.88 to 0.58 (over 30% less of each marginal deposit dollar converted to credit, the rest absorbed by reduced wholesale funding), while the recycling rate fell from 0.65 to 0.47 (fewer credit-generated payment flows return as stable deposits, consistent with declining deposit "specialness" as households/firms shift toward money-market funds and other non-bank instruments, plus a shift in bank asset composition from loans toward securities). (3) Combining both margins, the *total* bank-credit effect of a $1 deposit inflow fell from $2.13 to $0.88 — less than half its former value. (4) Decomposing drivers: a higher capital wedge and reduced recycling each account for roughly half a dollar of the decline per deposit dollar; the liquidity wedge moves slightly in the opposite direction. Separately, Quantitative Easing lowers the marginal credit impact of a new deposit (reserves and deposits arrive together, expanding the balance sheet and tightening the capital constraint), while Quantitative Tightening can raise it (removing reserves without a commensurate deposit decline, tightening the liquidity constraint and raising the marginal value of stable funding). (5) Non-bank credit does not compensate: in equilibrium a deposit inflow reaches non-banks only via lower bank loan rates pulling borrowers away, so non-bank credit *contracts* rather than expands — and even the most favorable spillover scenario (all non-recycled proceeds flowing into money markets) would require an implausibly strong pass-through to restore the early-period credit response.

**Why it matters.** The paper reframes "deposits fund loans" vs. "loans create deposits" as two margins of a single, empirically quantifiable multiplier rather than competing theories, and shows that multiplier has weakened substantially — an "implicit narrowing" of banking driven by higher balance-sheet costs and non-bank competition rather than formal separation of deposit-taking from lending. This has direct implications for the strength of monetary and fiscal policy transmission through the banking system, and for how much migration of deposits toward non-bank alternatives (money-market funds, stablecoins) should actually be expected to matter for aggregate credit supply.

**Deposit-franchise link.** The recycling rate is effectively a structural measure of deposit-franchise durability from the *credit-creation* side: a bank's own lending replenishes its deposit franchise only to the extent loan proceeds resettle as stable deposits, and that channel's decline (0.65→0.47) is direct evidence of eroding deposit specialness — complementing this session's demand-side stickiness papers with a supply-side, general-equilibrium view of why deposit franchise value has been shrinking.
