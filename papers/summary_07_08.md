# Deposit Specialization and Lending Behavior

**Kristian Blickle, Cecilia Parlatore, and Anthony Saunders (Federal Reserve Bank of New York Staff Report No. 1175, December 2025; revised July 2026)**

**Research question.** Does the *type* of depositor a bank serves (retail vs. corporate vs. NBFI) — not just the coarse insured/uninsured split used in prior work — systematically shape the asset side of the bank's balance sheet, its loan terms, and its response to funding shocks?

**Data/methods.** The authors merge four confidential supervisory sources for the ~25 largest U.S. banks (accounting for the bulk of C&I lending and roughly $11–13 trillion in deposits): FR 2052a (daily/monthly liquidity monitoring, with deposits broken out by counterparty type x insured status x product x maturity), Y-14 Schedule H (loan-level C&I lending, 3.3 million loan-quarter observations), Y-14 Schedule B (securities holdings), and Y-9C (aggregate balance-sheet and real-estate data). They classify banks by their plurality depositor type (retail, corporate, or NBFI) as of January 2020, run bank-level and loan-level regressions of asset composition/loan terms on deposit shares (with bank and time fixed effects), and exploit the COVID-19 deposit surge — largely driven by QE and stimulus rather than banks' own lending opportunities — as a quasi-exogenous shock to study how different bank types deploy new funding, linking outcomes to Compustat borrower data.

**Headline findings.**
1. Depositor specialization is stark and persistent: retail-oriented banks derive ~60%+ of deposits from retail customers with almost no NBFI exposure, and vice versa. Retail deposits are the most stable (lowest relative volatility even during 2020) and least rate-sensitive; NBFI/corporate deposits are far more rate-sensitive, with sensitivity spiking during COVID-era QE.
2. This funding structure maps directly onto assets: retail-funded banks hold *more* C&I lending and real estate exposure than corporate-oriented banks and pay markedly lower deposit rates (a one-standard-deviation rise in deposit-rate variability raises loan rates ~6bp; moving from the 25th to 75th percentile of retail deposit share adds roughly seven quarters of loan maturity). NBFI-oriented banks hold almost exclusively cash/Treasuries and barely lend.
3. Responses to the COVID deposit windfall were heterogeneous and shaped by pre-existing specialization: retail banks parked new deposits largely in real estate and cash; corporate banks converted new corporate deposits into C&I lending; NBFI banks did almost nothing with inflows. Firms borrowing from corporate-deposit-oriented banks grew assets and profitability more during COVID than those borrowing from retail-oriented banks.
4. A novel risk index (asset duration ÷ liability maturity) shows most banks maintain prudent buffers, but Silicon Valley Bank stands out as the one clear pre-failure outlier with a ratio far above peers — validating the measure as an early-warning tool.

**Why it matters / contribution.** The paper shows banks are not interchangeable conduits for monetary policy or credit-market interventions: the real effects of a liquidity injection (e.g., QE, deposit insurance policy, stimulus) depend on *which* banks receive the inflow, because asset and liability structures are jointly and persistently determined. This has direct implications for the transmission of monetary policy and for prudential/macroprudential design (e.g., the narrow-bank debate, LCR calibration), since coarse insured/uninsured classifications materially understate the heterogeneity that actually governs bank behavior.

**Deposit-franchise link.** This paper is a direct empirical anchor for the deposit-franchise-value literature (Drechsler, Savov, Schnabl): it shows that franchise value from sticky, low-beta retail deposits is not just a funding-cost advantage but translates one-for-one into longer-duration, lower-rate, more real-estate-intensive lending — the flip side being that volatile, rate-sensitive NBFI/corporate deposit franchises fund almost no long-term credit at all.

# Bank Market Power

**Luigi Bocola, Gideon Bornstein, Cedomir Malgieri, and Federico Puglisi (July 2026)**

**Research question.** How much market power do banks exercise in *lending* (as opposed to deposit-taking), and what are the macroeconomic consequences — including how capital-requirement policy interacts with bank market structure?

**Data/methods.** Using AnaCredit (the euro-area credit register, loan-level, 2019Q3) and the longer Italian Credit Register/TAXIA panel (2005–2019), the authors first residualize overdraft interest rates on default-risk and collateral indicators (which explain 26% of dispersion), then run an AKM-style two-way fixed-effects decomposition (firm FE + bank-province FE) identified from firms borrowing simultaneously from multiple banks — interpreting the bank fixed effect as a deviation from the law of one price. They then build a general-equilibrium model (in the Gertler-Kiyotaki/Karadi tradition) with oligopolistic banks, leverage constraints, random firm-bank matching, and CES-imperfect-substitutability across lenders, calibrated via indirect inference to match loan-demand semi-elasticities (estimated via an Amiti-Weinstein shift-share instrument), bank market-share concentration, and average bank-firm relationships.

**Headline findings.**
1. Bank fixed effects explain 15–20% of residual interest-rate dispersion (17% in AnaCredit 2019Q3, ~20% in the longer Italian series) — roughly half the explanatory power of default risk/collateral — and this share fell during the pre-crisis 2000s liberalization and rose again after the 2008/2012 crises as the industry consolidated (top-5 bank share in local markets rose from ~57% in 2005 to 78% in 2019).
2. Bank fixed effects are strongly, robustly correlated with local market share (positive and concave: beta1 ~ 1.65 to 4.4, beta2 negative) even after controlling for funding costs, bank balance-sheet characteristics, and time/bank fixed effects — and negatively correlated with the number of competing lenders a bank's borrowers have access to, confirming a market-power (not just cost-heterogeneity) interpretation.
3. Estimated loan-demand semi-elasticities are large (total elasticity ~ -19; substitution-share elasticity ~ -12), consistent with recent U.S. and euro-area estimates; the calibrated oligopoly model matches the data's AKM decomposition (24% vs. 17-20% bank-FE share) as an untargeted moment.
4. Counterfactuals: eliminating bank markups (pricing at marginal cost) raises output by 1.3% on impact but only ~0.4–0.5% in the long run, because lower bank profits erode capital accumulation and tighten leverage constraints over time — a genuine short-run/long-run tradeoff. Separately, halving capital requirements (theta: 8%->4%) lowers average loan rates by up to 83bp, with roughly half of that decline coming from reduced markups (via reallocation of market share from large to small banks and reduced concentration) rather than from lower marginal funding costs — highlighting an underappreciated pro-competitive channel of capital regulation.

**Why it matters / contribution.** The paper provides the first credit-register-based measurement strategy that cleanly ties AKM-style bank fixed effects to deviations from the law of one price and to market concentration, and embeds this in a quantitative oligopoly model that can be used to evaluate macroprudential policy. It shows that market structure (not just balance-sheet mechanics) is a first-order determinant of how capital regulation transmits to lending rates.

**Deposit-franchise link.** While Bocola et al. focus on *loan-side* market power rather than deposit-side franchise value, the paper's mechanism is the mirror image of the deposit franchise story: just as sticky, low-beta deposits generate rents on the liability side, concentrated local loan markets generate markup rents on the asset side, and the model shows these rents are structurally linked to bank capitalization/leverage constraints — directly complementary to a session on how funding-side franchise value and asset-side market power jointly determine bank profitability and credit supply.
