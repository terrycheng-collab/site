# Bye Bye Beta: Deposit Duration with Fixed Spreads (Robert Rogers, U Chicago)

**Question.** Do very low policy rates actually destroy bank value, or were banks/analysts who feared "rate normalization" simply wrong? DeMarzo, Krishnamurthy, and Nagel (2024) show bank cash flows decompose into positive fixed and floating streams, implying value should rise monotonically as rates fall — yet SVB-style behavior suggests otherwise.

**Data/method.** Rogers fits a censored AR(1) model of deposit rates on the risk-free rate using FFIEC call-report data, 1987–2023, testing a "fixed spread" model (deposit rate = risk-free rate minus a constant, floored at zero) against the standard "fixed beta" model. He then prices deposits as interest-rate caps using a calibrated LIBOR market model (Brace-Gątarek-Musiela) matched to traded cap prices, and validates the resulting duration measure against bank-stock responses to high-frequency FOMC shocks, 2002–2023.

**Findings.** (1) Long-run pass-through is near-complete at high rates but collapses toward zero at the lower bound — a fixed ~2pp spread fits far better than a constant beta (β≈1, δ≈2%, R²=0.997, vs. R²=0.93 for a linear model). (2) Valued as caps rather than linear liabilities, deposit franchise value is hump-shaped in the rate level, peaking near 2%; duration turns negative at very low rates even with no runoff or fixed costs. (3) This duration measure predicts FOMC-day bank stock returns with the correct sign and roughly correct magnitude (coefficient ≈1, R²≈10% excluding the financial crisis), while comparable measures from DeMarzo et al., Begenau-Piazzesi-Schneider, and Haddad-Sraer's income gap get the sign wrong. (4) The framework explains why banks piled into long-duration MBS/securities in 2020–21 (share of >15y assets rising from ~7% to over 11%) — a hedge against rates staying persistently low.

**Why it matters.** It resolves whether the deposit franchise hedges or amplifies asset-side interest-rate risk (the answer: both, depending on the rate level) and supplies regulators/analysts a duration measure that actually tracks realized bank-equity sensitivity to monetary shocks, unlike existing asset-focused measures.

**Deposit-franchise link:** demonstrates that franchise value and duration are *non-monotonic* in the rate level — the zero lower bound compresses deposit spreads and can flip deposit "stickiness" from a hedge into a source of interest-rate risk.

# How Do Banks Compete? Evidence from Advertising Videos (Xugan Chen, Allen Hu, Song Ma)

**Question.** Banks are known to differ in deposit/loan pricing and market power, but what concrete, observable actions do they take to build and defend that franchise value? The paper uses TV advertising content as a revealed-strategy lens on non-price competition.

**Data/method.** 51,349 Nielsen Ad Intel video clips (2004–2020) from 2,739 institutions, merged with Call Reports, FDIC Summary of Deposits, HMDA, and CRA data. The methodological contribution is an unsupervised video-embedding pipeline (multimodal OmniEmbed/Qwen2.5-Omni-7B embeddings → PaCMAP dimension reduction → hierarchical agglomerative clustering) that sorts ad content into pricing, service-quality, and trust/emotional-appeal categories without human labels. Identification comes from county-year fixed-effects panels, quasi-experiments (branch entry, bank M&A, FinTech entry, the 2016 Wells Fargo scandal), a Drechsler-Savov-Schnabl (2017)-style deposits-channel design, and a DMA border-discontinuity design.

**Findings.** Only 18% of ad content is pricing-related; 29% is service, 53% trust/emotion. High-market-share banks systematically downplay pricing and lean on service/trust content (a 10pp market-share increase cuts pricing mentions ~2.5% relative to the mean); new branch entrants advertise pricing and trust to overcome low market power and distrust, while M&A entrants (who inherit an existing customer base) instead ramp up service messaging (+7.8pp, ~21% increase). Ad content tracks bank fundamentals: pricing-heavy banks have higher deposit betas (weaker market power), service-heavy banks have higher deposit spreads. Following the deposits-channel design, banks with service/trust-oriented advertising raise deposit spreads more and contract their balance sheets more when the Fed funds rate rises — i.e., advertising-built franchise value amplifies the deposits channel of monetary transmission. The border-discontinuity design confirms this is causally identified, not just correlational, for deposit growth, deposit spreads, and mortgage/small-business loan origination.

**Why it matters.** This is arguably the first micro-evidence that a specific, budgeted bank action (TV advertising, ~9.5% of marketing budgets) actively constructs deposit franchise value and shapes monetary policy pass-through, complementing balance-sheet-based market-power measures with a directly observable competitive strategy.

**Deposit-franchise link:** advertising is shown to be a direct *input* into deposit-franchise value and stickiness — it is how banks manufacture the switching costs and trust that let them widen spreads during tightening, the behavioral counterpart to the franchise-value fragility documented in the Rogers paper.
