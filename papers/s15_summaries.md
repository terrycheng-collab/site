# The Spatial Distribution of Markups (Jonathan Becker, Chris Edmond, Virgiliu Midrigan, Daniel Yi Xu; June 2026)

**Question.** Does the geographic segmentation of the US economy matter for competition and market power? Or is the within-country spatial structure irrelevant, as most macro-markup models implicitly assume (frictionless internal trade) and most spatial models assume (constant markups)?

**Data/method.** A quantitative spatial GE model with heterogeneous multi-establishment firms, Atkeson–Burstein oligopolistic competition in each destination market, and endogenously variable markups; local wages clear local labor markets. The calibration covers about 220,000 US manufacturing firms in 363 six-digit NAICS sectors (about 600 firms per sector) across 170 BEA Economic Areas. Each model firm's establishments are placed exactly where the real firm's are (Census of Manufactures). Productivity dispersion and the correlation between productivity and establishment count match national sales concentration and multi-unit employment shares. Sector-specific iceberg trade costs exactly reproduce Commodity Flow Survey gravity regressions. The comparison model has no geography but is calibrated to the same national concentration facts.

**Findings.** (1) The aggregate markup is 1.26, ranging from 1.22 (greater New York) and 1.24 (Los Angeles) to 1.35 (Honolulu). Local sales HHI (about 0.15) sits between national sales HHI (0.10) and local production HHI (0.36). (2) Spatial frictions sharply limit competition. The no-geography model only matches the benchmark's markup and concentration after 90% of its firms are removed (about 60 per sector). Without geography, doubling or halving the number of firms has negligible effects on markups and misallocation. (3) The welfare cost of markups averages 5.8% (consumption-equivalent), versus 3.7% without geography. It is about 1% or less in New York and as much as 20% in Honolulu, so smaller, poorer, remote locations bear roughly 20 times the cost of central ones. (4) A 20% cut in trade costs lowers the aggregate markup only about 0.8%. Local markup declines range from 0.5% (New York) to 1.8–1.9% (rural Utah, Colorado, Kansas), and consumption gains from about 1.6% (New York) to nearly 16% (remote Dakotas). (5) Falling trade costs raise national concentration while lowering local concentration, reproducing the Rossi-Hansberg–Sarte–Trachter "diverging trends." Here that pattern means convergence toward an integrated market, with lower markups and higher productivity. (6) Adding worker mobility via amenity preferences leaves welfare costs almost unchanged.

**Why it matters.** It overturns the "elusive pro-competitive effects" result (Arkolakis et al.; Edmond–Midrigan–Xu 2015). With about 600 firms per industry, standard models predict small pro-competitive gains, but spatial frictions leave many locations with only a handful of *effective* competitors. National concentration is therefore a poor guide to market power, and infrastructure or internal-trade policy can have large pro-competitive payoffs concentrated in remote regions.

**Misallocation link:** the spatial structure of the economy is itself a source of markup dispersion, and therefore of misallocation. Ignoring geography understates the aggregate welfare cost of market power by more than a third (3.7% versus 5.8%), and they are distributed very unequally across space.

# More Than the Sum of Its Parts? Markups and the Role of Establishments (Markus Kondziella, Joshua Weiss; June 2026)

**Question.** The misallocation-from-markups literature treats firm production as one-dimensional and concludes that large, high-markup firms produce too little and should be subsidized. What changes when firms also choose an extensive margin (how many establishments to open) and market power is set at the establishment level?

**Data/method.** Universe of Swedish services firms and establishments, 1997–2017 (Statistics Sweden: Företagens Ekonomi financial accounts merged with monthly employer–employee records), with production-function markups. The model has firms competing through establishments. Each firm has a baseline quality and draws additional establishment opportunities (quality plus opening cost), and Kimball demand operates across *establishments* rather than firms, so larger establishments face lower elasticities. Theorems characterize distortions against a planner with fixed labor. The quantitative version is calibrated to the Swedish facts and used to compute the first best and optimal revenue-neutral firm-size-dependent tax schedules T(S).

**Findings.** (1) Multi-establishment firms are rare but earn half of all sales. (2) Markups rise with sales per establishment but *not* with the number of establishments, so market power is establishment-level. (3) Each successive establishment is smaller relative to its municipality-industry, controlling for age, so the marginal establishment is the smallest. (4) Theory: firms undervalue production at large versus small establishments, opening large versus small establishments, and producing at existing versus opening new ones. With firm-level market power, by contrast, high-markup firms uniformly underproduce, and with constant elasticities the equilibrium is efficient. (5) The first best raises consumption 0.81%, mainly by shifting 2.4% of labor from opening establishments into production. (6) The optimal size-dependent policy holding establishments fixed subsidizes large firms and removes 98% of intensive-margin misallocation (+0.41% of the possible 0.42%). Once establishments respond, it *backfires* (−0.76%) as large firms open too many establishments. (7) The optimal policy that accounts for the extensive margin is non-monotonic. It taxes the smallest and largest firms, subsidizes large single-establishment (medium-sized) firms, and recovers only 0.05% (6% of the losses).

**Why it matters.** It reverses the policy lesson of Edmond–Midrigan–Xu-type models for sectors (like services) where firms grow by adding outlets. A firm's markup says it underproduces at existing outlets but says little about whether it has too many or too few outlets. The latter can dominate, making it optimal to *tax* the largest, highest-markup firms.

**Misallocation link:** misallocation has two margins, output per unit and number of units, and market power distorts them in opposite directions for the same firm. Size-dependent correctives designed for one margin can worsen total misallocation.

# Market Concentration and Aggregate Productivity: The Role of Demand (Jeremy Pearce, Liangjie Wu; August 2026)

**Question.** Large firms hold share through both productivity and demand investment (marketing), and they spend more on the latter (4.2% versus 2.4% of revenue for R&D). Is strategic demand investment under oligopoly efficient or distortionary, how does it shape concentration and aggregate productivity, and how do size-based policies interact with it?

**Data/method.** Firm-level sales-and-marketing expenditure for US public firms, capitalized into a customer-capital stock and measured alongside labor productivity. The construct is corroborated with NielsenIQ scanner data using the Hottman–Redding–Weinstein residual-demand measure. The model is a dynamic Cournot oligopoly under CES demand (microfounded with rational inattention) with two dynamic state variables per firm, productivity and customer capital, solved globally with a new method that scales to empirically relevant firm counts. A Hsieh–Klenow-style wedge accounting uses customer capital in place of capital, followed by planner and policy counterfactuals.

**Findings.** (1) Facts: customer capital correlates with revenue about 0.75, versus about 0.50 for productivity. It is more persistent (AR(1) 0.98 versus 0.86), marketing-investment growth is positively autocorrelated, and markets have 4–7 effective competitors. (2) Because markups rise with share, marketing's return peaks for firms already ahead, the opposite of the neck-and-neck logic of Aghion et al. This creates a *legacy effect*: firms defend accumulated demand after their productivity edge fades, so equally productive firms can sustain persistent size gaps. (3) Equalizing the revenue product of customer capital would raise output 52% (41.9 log points). Of that, 44% comes from adjustment costs, 11% from the legacy effect (4.7 log points, about 5% of aggregate productivity, recoverable by a planner who corrects only the strategic incentive), and 45% is residual. (4) On net marketing lowers welfare: shutting it down lowers frontier productivity 4.9% but raises welfare 5.2%, because the concentration, markups, and labor it absorbs outweigh the sorting gain. (5) Output subsidies help when small but entrench low-productivity incumbents when large.

**Why it matters.** It separates "good" (productivity-driven) from "bad" (demand-driven) concentration, which look identical in shares and markups, and warns that the leading policy response to markup misallocation (size-based subsidies) can deepen a dynamic distortion. Competition policy needs to diagnose the *source* of concentration.

**Misallocation link:** introduces customer capital as a misallocated input, with dispersion in its revenue product comparable in scale to classic capital misallocation. It traces part of that dispersion to strategic entrenchment rather than frictions or policy wedges.

# Variable Markups, Incomplete Pass-Throughs, and R&D Misallocation (Mohamad Adhami, Jean-Félix Brouillette, Emma Rockall; March 2025 draft)

**Question.** Growth models typically use demand systems (CES, Bertrand/limit pricing) that contradict the firm-level evidence that markups rise with market share and cost pass-through is positive but incomplete. If the demand system matches this evidence, how do markups affect the level *and cross-firm allocation* of R&D, and what does that cost in growth?

**Data/method.** An endogenous-growth model building on Luttmer (2007) with Kimball demand. Monopolistically competitive firms control the drift of process efficiency via R&D (a controlled diffusion in the Ericson–Pakes tradition), pay overhead to stay active, and entrants imitate incumbents. Growth comes from incumbent innovation plus selective exit. The balanced growth path has a Pareto-tailed efficiency distribution solved as a PDE system (Lucas–Moll). Parameters are estimated by GMM on French administrative firm data, 2009–2019 (CASD), targeting the markup–market-share relationship and firm-size dispersion; average pass-through is untargeted but matches the data. Counterfactuals use size-dependent production subsidies that remove product-market distortions.

**Findings.** (1) The model endogenously generates higher markups for more productive firms and incomplete pass-through: cost reductions lower price, and demand becomes less elastic at lower prices. (2) The new mechanism is that ideas are nonrival within the firm, so returns to process innovation scale with output. Markups shrink scale, and with variable markups the shrinkage, and hence the private-versus-social R&D wedge, is largest for the most productive firms. (3) Removing markup distortions raises long-run TFP growth 1.2pp, through more aggregate R&D, reallocation of R&D toward large firms, and higher churn. (4) Holding aggregate R&D labor fixed, reallocation alone raises growth 0.92pp, nearly 75% of the total. (5) Correcting only the *level* of markups has muted effects because higher wages bid up R&D costs, while neutralizing markup *dispersion* delivers slightly more than the full intervention.

**Why it matters.** It moves the markup-misallocation literature (Edmond–Midrigan–Xu, Baqaee–Farhi) from static level effects to growth effects. The dynamic cost (nearly 1pp of growth per year) dwarfs typical static estimates and concentrates on the economy's most productive firms.

**Misallocation link:** markup dispersion acts as a size-dependent wedge in the Hsieh–Klenow sense, but on innovation effort. It misallocates R&D across firms, and the growth loss comes from dispersion rather than the average markup.

# Innovation and Pricing Frictions (Xiaojie Liu, Sara Moreira; February 2026)

**Question.** Standard macro treats nominal rigidities as short-lived and irrelevant for long-run outcomes. Do pricing frictions actually operate over product life cycles, and if so, how do they shape innovation, product churn, misallocation, and optimal R&D policy?

**Data/method.** NielsenIQ product–firm longitudinal scanner data for US consumer goods, with cohort-by-age price profiles (national and store-level), sector-level rigidity measures, and a quasi-experiment exploiting firms' exposure to import tariffs. The model is a Klette–Kortum growth model with endogenous varieties, money demand and trend inflation, and Calvo-style resets: firms choose the introductory price of each new product, which then re-prices only with probability γ. It is estimated on four moments: overshooting, incumbent and entrant innovation rates, and growth.

**Findings.** (1) New products enter 10–20% above the sector price level, a pattern pervasive across incumbents and entrants, novelty levels, and food and non-food. (2) Incumbent nominal prices are flat over the life cycle; relative prices fall about 2–3% a year over the first five years despite frequent temporary price changes. (3) Stickier sectors have larger entry premia. Tariff shocks have no detectable effect on incumbent prices but raise relative prices of new products launched later (a 0.10pp rise in exposure raises them about 0.14pp two years out). (4) The estimated γ implies about 9 years to reset to trend versus a 7-year average product life, so most products never re-price. (5) Overshooting insures against real-price erosion, and markups disperse across otherwise identical products, which is misallocation. Innovation both suffers (lower product values) and helps (more reset options, compressed markup distribution). (6) Shutting down overshooting costs 7% of consumption (−3.77 log points growth, −4.41 variety, +0.95 efficiency). (7) The optimal incumbent R&D subsidy is 41%: innovation rises from 12.7% to 17.2%, varieties rise 2%, and efficiency rises 0.34%. It is far more effective than an entrant subsidy (optimal 19%, which crowds out incumbents). The optimal incumbent subsidy rises with pricing frictions, and in the frictionless benchmark it falls to 38%.

**Why it matters.** It links nominal rigidities to long-run growth. Trend inflation and slow-moving price frictions affect innovation incentives, product turnover, and trade-shock pass-through, and R&D policy should be sector-specific in how sticky prices are.

**Misallocation link:** long-run pricing frictions create markup dispersion across identical products, a misallocation that innovation itself mitigates through product churn. This gives R&D subsidies a misallocation-reducing role on top of the usual growth rationale.

# Why Don't Old Firms Do New Things? (Nicolas Crouzet, Zhiguo He, Victor Lyonnet, Yueran Ma; November 2025 draft)

**Question.** Since Schumpeter, new technologies are associated with new firms, yet incumbents successfully adopt many (polymers, smartphones, cloud computing). When and why do new technologies *require* new firms? This is the neglected Coasian question of "which ideas can be implemented within a firm?"

**Data/method.** A model in which firms run business model A and a new model B emerges with a different optimal occupation mix. Each occupation carries O\*NET "workstyles," and larger workstyle gaps mean B's managers must more often override A's accumulated rules. That costs time and productivity, and older firms have more rules. Validation: Revelio employee reviews show that mentions of rules and excessive meetings rise with firm age. The key regressor is a predicted five-year industry workstyle change. Patent-text similarity to occupations' routine and nonroutine tasks (Kogan et al. 2024) predicts future industry occupation mix (BLS), which is mapped into an employment-weighted workstyle change (2003 onward). Outcomes: industry VC investment, Compustat valuation and five-year sales and employment growth interacted with log firm age, and Census BDS employment growth by age group (1–25).

**Findings.** (1) A one-s.d. larger predicted workstyle change raises industry VC investment about 0.3 log points, controlling for incumbent market cap. (2) Among public firms, young firms have higher valuations and faster realized sales and employment growth relative to old firms when the workstyle change is larger. (3) In the BDS, young age groups grow significantly faster than older ones under larger workstyle shifts. (4) The sheer volume of new technology (all patents, Kelly et al. breakthrough patents, Bowen–Frésard–Hoberg rapidly evolving patents) does not predict young-firm outperformance and does not absorb the workstyle effect. (5) Age effects are not driven by size or financial constraints.

**Why it matters.** Incumbents are not generically bad at new technology through slow learning or fear of cannibalization. They fail when the technology demands a different *organizational style*. This gives a measurable criterion for when entry, VC, and pro-entrepreneurship policy are especially valuable, and extends the boundary-of-the-firm literature beyond vertical integration.

**Misallocation link:** organizational frictions tie new technologies to the wrong firms. When a technology requires new workstyles, resources held by incumbents are poorly deployed, and reallocation toward young firms (and the entry and financing channels that enable it) is where the productivity gains come from.

# Ideas and Firm Dynamics When It Takes Two to Tango (Seula Kim, Jane Olmstead-Rumsey, Honghao Wang; August 2026)

**Question.** Patenting has shifted to teams, and inventors have concentrated in large firms. If teams form mostly within firm boundaries, does the rise of team production explain inventor concentration, and is the resulting allocation of inventors efficient given knowledge spillovers?

**Data/method.** USPTO PatentsView (1976–2018) linked to Revelio Labs/LinkedIn employment histories and Compustat. The model is a Hopenhayn/Melitz-style GE model of the inventor labor market. Within a firm, each inventor pair draws a bilateral collaboration productivity, and inventors can alternatively work solo, so expected inventor productivity rises concavely with coworkers in firms that adopt team production. Knowledge spillovers across firms depend on firm productivity and size (Chikis–Kleinman–Prato). Two steady states (1976–1999, 2000–2018) are estimated by direct and indirect inference, including patent-firm returns-to-scale estimates, and compared with a planner who chooses allocation, entry and exit, and team adoption.

**Findings.** (1) Team patents rose from about 45% to more than 80% of private US patents. About 90% of team collaborations are within the same firm. Inventor employment concentration rose six times more than overall employment concentration. (2) Team-producing firms have less-decreasing returns to scale in inventors, and this advantage grew over time, consistent with increasing knowledge specialization. (3) The estimated change in idea production raises concentration and average firm size as in the data and raises innovative output, but endogenously lowers spillovers. Idea-production changes explain 92% of the spillover decline. (4) The competitive equilibrium's innovative-output shortfall versus the planner grows from 0.2% (early) to 1.7% (late), about 1.5% under an alternative calibration. That is the "six-fold" rise in misallocation, and about 73% of the widening is due to the idea-production shift alone. Late-period spillovers are about 6% below the planner's. (5) Planner wedges fall with firm productivity and steepened over time, so the first best needs larger R&D subsidies to small firms and more entry subsidies now than before.

**Why it matters.** It offers a micro-founded, organizational explanation for inventor concentration and the decline in knowledge diffusion from large to small firms, and possibly for "ideas getting harder to find." It also implies that R&D policy should tilt toward small firms and entrants.

**Misallocation link:** the misallocated input is inventors. Because firms do not internalize spillovers and teams raise large firms' returns to scale, inventors flow excessively from small, spillover-rich firms to large ones, and technological change amplified that wedge.

# Expectations versus Reality in Business Formation (Emin Dinlersoz, Yueyuan Ma; July 2026)

**Question.** How accurate are would-be entrepreneurs' expectations about whether they will become employers and how many workers they will hire, and do belief errors distort entry and the allocation of resources at business formation?

**Data/method.** About 17 million US new-business applications, 2017–2021 (IRS Form SS-4 EIN filings underlying the Census Business Formation Statistics), with reported expected initial employment, linked to the Longitudinal Business Database for realized employer entry and employment. Tools: forecast-error decompositions on the extensive and intensive margins, Mincer–Zarnowitz tests, correlates (legal form, tract demographics, local firm density and dispersion, sector), COVID-era dynamics, and third-year outcomes. The model extends Jovanovic (1982) with informative pre-application signals, heterogeneous priors with an uninformative component, learning, and selection into application and entry, and yields closed-form entry distortions relative to a common-prior counterfactual.

**Findings.** (1) Applicants overestimate employment on average, mainly because many expect entry but never become employers. Those who expect and achieve entry typically underestimate. (2) Mincer–Zarnowitz slopes are 0.29 (all) and 0.26 (positive expectations) with positive intercepts, strongly rejecting unbiased, efficient forecasts. Forecast efficiency declined over 2017–2021, especially after 2019. (3) Corporations and LLCs are better calibrated than sole proprietors and partnerships. College share and local firm density reduce overestimation. Non-white share and incumbent dispersion increase it. High-tech and manufacturing overestimate more; professional services and real estate less. (4) Conditional on first-year employment, greater initial overestimation predicts *higher* third-year employment, so there is private information absent from Jovanovic and Hopenhayn benchmarks. (5) Belief noise generates net excess *expected* entry of 8.5–9.5% of applications but net *forgone actual* employer entry of 0.5–0.9%, and the latter roughly doubled 2017–2021. Noise draws low-quality applicants in and deters some high-quality ones.

**Why it matters.** It provides the first large-scale observational link between ex-ante entrepreneurial expectations and outcomes. Application surges (such as post-2020) may overstate real employer formation, and application-stage expectations are informative early indicators of job creation.

**Misallocation link:** misallocation at the entry margin. Noisy, heterogeneous beliefs sort the wrong potential entrants into and out of business formation, so fewer high-quality employers form even as applications rise.

# Innovate to Borrow: How Intangibles Shape Finance and Growth (Vladimir Asriyan, Alberto Martin, Guillermo Ordoñez, Victoria Vanasco; May 2026 preliminary draft)

**Question.** As investment shifts to firm-specific intangibles and lending shifts toward cash flows, what determines borrowing capacity when assets have little resale value, and how do financial frictions interact with firms' *choice* of technology?

**Data/method.** Pure theory (no quantification yet). A dynamic GE macro-finance model in the Hart–Moore / Kiyotaki–Moore tradition. Heterogeneous-productivity entrepreneurs cannot pledge cash flows (inalienable human capital) and choose between generic technologies using redeployable physical capital and innovative technologies built on intangibles accumulated through learning (know-how) or experimentation (R&D). On default, lenders seize all assets, including non-redeployable ones, and renegotiation bargaining power shapes default payoffs. Asset prices close the GE feedback, and the analysis includes normative and policy work.

**Findings.** (1) Borrowing capacity depends on technology choice, not just liquidation values. Intangibles raise the continuation value, making default costly and supporting repayment contingent on success, which microfounds cash-flow-based lending and VC reliance. (2) Technology choice is non-monotonic in productivity. Low types innovate to experiment (hoard cash, delay investment), middle types choose generic technologies at full collateral capacity, and high types innovate to scale and relax constraints further. (3) Without frictions only the most productive are active, and they experiment, with capital flowing only to successful projects. Frictions impede reallocation, lower the value of experimentation, and push firms toward generic or innovate-to-scale choices, so experimentation is insufficient. The cause is not intangibles' pledgeability but the cost of reallocating capital. (4) The model predicts that financially deep economies have more experimentation and fast scaling by young firms, while constrained economies concentrate R&D in large firms, consistent with cross-country evidence. (5) Policy: forcing or subsidizing innovation can backfire (wrong type of innovation, wrong entrepreneurs), while a Pigouvian tax on generic investment lowers asset prices and improves allocation.

**Why it matters.** It challenges the view that intangibles tighten financing constraints and redirects policy (notably Europe's innovation debate) from funding early-stage innovation toward enabling capital reallocation to scale winners.

**Misallocation link:** financial frictions misallocate on two margins at once, capital across entrepreneurs *and* technologies across entrepreneurs. The technology-misallocation margin is new, and it means standard capital-wedge measures miss part of the cost.

# Misallocation due to Incomplete Markets (David R. Baqaee, Ariel Burstein; July 2026, v4)

**Question.** How much of the economy's resources are wasted because households cannot fully share risk or smooth consumption, within and across countries, measured without a social welfare function or interpersonal comparisons?

**Data/method.** A Debreu (1951) "distance to the frontier" measure, building on Baqaee–Burstein (2025a): the maximal proportional contraction of the consumption possibility set (equivalently TFP) that keeps everyone at least as well off as the status quo. It is a multi-agent Lucas (1987) consumption equivalent that is invariant to monotone utility transformations and accommodates heterogeneous preferences. The authors derive exact certainty-equivalent formulas (closed-economy baseline: CE of aggregate consumption over the sum of household CEs), extensions with labor-leisure and capital, and second-order Harberger-triangle approximations. These need only consumption panels, prices, the EIS, the risk-free rate, and internationally a static input-output table and trade elasticities; income processes, portfolios, and friction details are not needed. Applications: a Bewley calibration, PSID household consumption, and a 32-country × 54-industry WIOD model with 1970–2019 macro data, plus Monte Carlo accuracy checks.

**Findings.** (1) Imperfect insurance of idiosyncratic risk destroys about 20% of aggregate US consumption in every date and state, in both the Bewley calibration and the PSID. That is about three orders of magnitude larger than Lucas's cost of aggregate volatility, and the triangle approximation is very accurate even far from the approximation point. (2) The efficiency gain is about half the veil-of-ignorance "distance to first best," which also penalizes inequality. (3) With labor-leisure choice, Benabou (2002)-style efficiency measures can prefer more unequal (even Pareto-inefficient) allocations; the proposed measure avoids this. (4) Incomplete international markets cost about 5% of world consumption, and input-output linkages matter quantitatively. Excluding fast growers (China, India, Indonesia, South Korea) drops this to about 1%, so gains come from intertemporal trade with fast growers, not risk-sharing among similar economies. (5) Losses rise with a lower EIS, a lower interest rate, and a higher Armington elasticity.

**Why it matters.** It gives a portable, low-data way to put incomplete-markets inefficiency on the same scale as production misallocation. The domestic cost is large and the cross-border risk-sharing cost among rich countries is small, which reorients where financial-completion gains lie.

**Misallocation link:** extends the wedge-and-triangle machinery of production misallocation (Hsieh–Klenow, Baqaee–Farhi) to the dynamic, stochastic misallocation of *consumption* across households, states, and time, and finds it plausibly larger than most production-side estimates.
