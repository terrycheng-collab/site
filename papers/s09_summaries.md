# Generous Long-Term Contracts (Sylvain Chassang, Princeton)
*Draft of January 24, 2026*

**Question.** Firms often offer a menu, a safe baseline contract plus a new higher-powered one (variable-price electricity, high-deductible health plans). Consumers who can't tell which option will suit them seldom pick the new one. EDF's "Tempo" variable-price electricity contract has about 3% take-up, even though industry data suggest nearly all households would save about 20% on it without changing how they consume. Can a single contract get the efficiency gains of the menu without depending on consumers to choose from it?

**Model/approach.** A dynamic principal–agent model without discounting over N periods. Each period the consumer sees a public state k_t and a private preference α_t, then chooses consumption q_t. Transfers follow a baseline contract τ⁰ or an alternative τ¹. Skepticism is modeled as a sharp stance: if τ¹ does not dominate τ⁰, the consumer judges the menu with an ambiguity-averse (adversarial) stance. If it does dominate, the consumer uses the shared prior. The proposed **generous long-term contract** τ̂ makes sure that at every horizon, the consumer's cumulative payments equal what they would have paid under whichever contract turns out best for them in hindsight. So τ̂ dominates τ⁰ by construction. The paper follows a "minimalist design" approach: it takes the existing menu as given rather than redesigning incentives from scratch.

**Findings.**
1. **Prop. 1 (conditionally i.i.d. environments).** Under τ̂, consumer welfare is at least the menu's, and for N large enough producer profit comes within any η of the profit from the menu when a rational expected-utility consumer evaluates it. Errors average out over a long relationship, so a hindsight-best guarantee costs the producer little.
2. **Prop. 2 (fast learning).** The result carries over to non-stationary settings in which the consumer quickly learns which contract suits them.
3. **Slow learning.** Here menus and generous contracts can produce different outcomes. The paper gives a partial fix for **Pareto alignments**, where τ¹ is a weighted average of τ⁰ and the producer's profit gain over a counterfactual profit bound π⁰(k). Prop. 3 shows the menu then gives a tight lower bound on producer gains relative to consumer gains (echoing linear-contract robustness results in Chassang 2013, Carroll 2015, and Madarász–Prat). Prop. 4 shows the generous version achieves that bound up to a penalty P_N ≤ M/√N.
4. **Calibration: French load shedding.** The data are RTE/EDF's simulated panel of 10,000 households with hourly consumption and local temperature. Persistent individual heterogeneity explains most of the variation in consumption: dynamic adjusted R² is 0.10 with pooled fixed effects, 0.53 with individual fixed effects, and 0.67 with individual FE plus AR(1,24) errors. If counterfactual profit is estimated from temperature alone, the penalty exceeds a 10% profit upside at inflation factor c = 0, and pushing it down to about 2% needs c ≈ 50%, which kills most of the efficiency gain. An individualized expanding-mean profit ratio gives a penalty of about 5% at c = 0 and well under 1% at c = 10%.
5. **Health-insurance application.** At a large US firm (Brot-Goldberg et al. 2017), 85% of employees pick the low-deductible PPO. The paper proposes a Pareto-aligned alternative that rewards cost reductions measured against a diagnosis-based benchmark, offered in generous form, instead of dropping the PPO altogether.

**Why it matters.** The paper gives a practical design fix for low take-up of efficient but risky-looking contracts. It rests on a clean theoretical point: over a long relationship, a hindsight-best guarantee is nearly free for the producer. The design lesson is that the guarantee only works if firms build individualized counterfactual-profit benchmarks.

**Dynamic games, contracts & markets link:** the core point is dynamic. A long contract horizon (√N averaging) lets a producer offer ex-post guarantees that would be ruinous in a one-shot contract. Commitment to a long-term relationship substitutes for the consumer's sophistication.

# Duality and Recursion in Dynamic Games (David M. Rahman, Minnesota)
*Draft of December 2025*

**Question.** Solving repeated and dynamic games with imperfect monitoring numerically is hard. The Abreu–Pearce–Stacchetti set-valued operators are often intractable and are not contractions. Existing fast algorithms cover only pure strategies with perfect monitoring (Abreu–Brooks–Sannikov 2020) or Brownian games with special structure (Sannikov 2007). Can dynamic programming be rebuilt around duality, with welfare weights as the state variable instead of continuation values?

**Model/approach.** A finite stage game with i.i.d. public signals and two equilibrium concepts:
- **ppRE** (perfect public randomization equilibrium): pure strategies plus public randomization.
- **ppCE** (perfect public correlated equilibrium): a mediator sends private, short-lived recommendations. Rationality is required after player deviations but not after mediator deviations, which is what keeps the problem linear.

The paper's chain of results runs as follows:
- **Thm 3.1:** extremal equilibrium welfare equals a linear-programming "primal sequence problem."
- **Thm 3.2:** no duality gap with the dual sequence problem.
- **Thm 4.1:** the primal functional equation, a finite-dimensional convex program (not a contraction, with multiple fixed points), has a maximal fixed point equal to the value function.
- **Thm 4.2:** a dual functional equation has the same value.
- **Thm 4.3:** the unfolded dual solution solves the dual sequence problem in a weighted-ℓ¹ multiplier space, since a uniform Slater condition fails when probabilities along the event tree shrink geometrically.
- **Thm 6.1:** a convergent value- and policy-iteration algorithm.

**Findings.**
1. **Kinks and flats.** Primal flats (dual kinks) are lotteries over continuation utilities, i.e. concavification. Dual flats (primal kinks) are lotteries over continuation welfare weights.
2. **Repeated Prisoners' Dilemma** with two signals, where pairwise identifiability fails, and δ = 0.975. **Claim 6.1:** the sum of payoffs in any ppRE is ≤ 1, so mutual cooperation is impossible because both players must be punished after a bad signal. The mediator's secret recommendations in ppCE restore mutual cooperation. Value iteration converges monotonically, and policy iteration speeds it up a lot.
3. **Optimal dynamic contracting.** Welfare weights follow a martingale, which generalizes Rogerson's inverse Euler equation to many agents. Prop. 6.1 gives the dual functional equation for the principal–risk-averse-agent problem. It generalizes smooth pasting at the endogenous retirement boundaries, where optimal lotteries (primal flats) appear. An extension with endogenous costly monitoring finds that the principal monitors to avoid costly firing, not to punish. In ppCE she can monitor secretly and hence rarely, while in ppRE she has to monitor publicly.
4. **Robustness.** A dual solution implies bounded steepness of the primal value function, so welfare is Lipschitz in incentive-compatibility errors. This extends Fudenberg–Levine (1986) on approximate equilibrium.
5. **Frequent actions (Prop. 7.1).** In the Brownian and Poisson limits, the dual equation becomes a "welfare optimality" HJB that links the curvature of welfare to welfare itself. The paper also sketches extensions to private signals and stochastic games.

**Why it matters.** It offers a unified, computable toolkit for dynamic games and contracts that allows correlation, imperfect monitoring and non-contraction operators. It also compares communication protocols: public randomization versus a mediator.

**Dynamic games, contracts & markets link:** this is the session's methods paper. A single dual recursive machinery covers repeated games (collusion), dynamic moral hazard contracts, and monitoring design, the building blocks behind most of the other papers in the session.

# Long-Term Contracts, Commitment, and Optimal Information Disclosure (Alessandro Dovis, Penn; Paolo Martellini, NYU Stern)
*Draft of October 2025 (first version March 2024)*

**Question.** Incumbent firms such as banks, insurers and employers learn about their customers over time and gain an informational monopoly over them. How much of that information should regulation force them to share with competitors? This is the question behind GDPR data portability, EU open banking and the CFPB's 2024 Personal Financial Data Rights Rule.

**Model/approach.** A two-period economy. Risk-averse consumers have Markov high/low income and trade with competitive risk-neutral firms. The incumbent learns first-period income, while outsiders see only a public signal from a disclosure policy (the information-design choice). Existence of equilibrium in pure strategies relies on Netzer–Scheuer-style costly contract withdrawal. The paper compares one-sided commitment (firms commit to long-term contracts, consumers can switch) with two-sided lack of commitment, then adds spot-only economies and outsiders that deliver exogenous utility benefits Δ (e.g. fintech entrants).

**Findings.**
1. **Prop. 1 (commitment).** No disclosure is optimal. Disclosure raises high types' outside option and tightens their participation constraint, which cuts the cross-subsidy to low types (a Hirshleifer effect). Consumption is downward-rigid and back-loaded, as in Harris–Holmström and Thomas–Worrall.
2. **No commitment.** Low types never get a cross-subsidy under any disclosure policy. Outsiders know that anyone they attract must be a low type, because the informed incumbent retains high types without distortion.
3. **Prop. 2.** Below a cutoff share of high types π*, no disclosure is optimal. Above it, the optimal policy is a **bad-news signal**: all high types and some low types get "good," and "bad" fully reveals a low type. This equalizes high types' consumption across the two periods. Pooling lowers high types' outside option and raises the incumbent's ex-post profits, which competition rebates up front. Consumption becomes upward-rigid and front-loaded, the inverse of the Harris–Holmström profile.
4. **Prop. 3 (long-term vs. spot).** Long-term relationships are better when the share of high types is near 0. When high types dominate, spot contracts are better and banning long-term relationships helps consumers, because spot markets allow cross-subsidization.
5. **Prop. 4 (productive outsiders).** Full disclosure is never optimal. The optimal amount of information rises with Δ only when disclosure is needed to get high types to switch; otherwise it falls to preserve cross-subsidization.
6. **Lending application.** Lenders start with low rates and raise them later, consistent with Ioannidou–Ongena. Borrowers who switch are adversely selected, consistent with Di Maggio–Yao's evidence on fintech borrowers. Unrestricted open banking, even on an opt-in basis, is never optimal, so giving consumers ownership of their data is not optimal. Credit ratings should instead pool high types with some low types, and a commitment right to data erasure can help when spot contracts are optimal.

**Why it matters.** The paper sets out a disciplined welfare framework for data-sharing regulation. It shows the answer depends on commitment and on the composition of the borrower pool, and it pushes back on the presumption that more data portability is always pro-consumer.

**Dynamic games, contracts & markets link:** this is a model of how limited commitment in long-term contracts interacts with dynamic competition and with information design by a regulator. Whether firms can commit decides whether disclosure is harmful, beneficial, or irrelevant.

# Dilutive Financing (Hanjoon Ryu, Singapore Management University)
*Draft of September 20, 2026 (first circulated March 2024)*

**Question.** Firms raise external money in lumps rather than continuously, and often raise it "early," before internal funds run out. The standard explanation is the Baumol–Tobin fixed transaction cost. Can financier bargaining power alone explain both patterns, with no fixed costs at all?

**Model/approach.** A firm needs funds repeatedly (for shocks or investment) and pays a carry cost φ on internal funds. It can bargain with a financier at any time and there is no fixed cost. If bargaining fails, however, the firm reaches an alternative financier only at Poisson rate γ. Because failure is costly, the bargaining surplus includes the value of avoiding delay, so financing dilutes firm value even though bargaining always succeeds in equilibrium. The model has a two-period core, an infinite-horizon version with a unique Markov-perfect equilibrium in (s,S) form (a funding target h̄ and a financing threshold h̲), covers both startups (σ = 0) and operating firms (Brownian cash flows), and adds an investment extension calibrated to Bolton–Chen–Wang (2011).

**Findings.**
1. **Prop. 1.** Financing is lumpy and intermittent. Firms raise more than they need so they bargain, and get diluted, less often.
2. **Early financing.** Firms may bargain while they still hold slack, so that a failed negotiation is less damaging (they don't have their "back against the wall").
3. **Lemma 3 (endogenous financing costs).** Firms that wait until funds hit zero pay rents of (1−θ)(V(h̄) − h̄), which look like fixed costs that scale with firm value. Firms that finance early pay rents of about (φ/γ)·Δh, which look like variable costs proportional to the amount raised and **do not depend on bargaining power θ**. Observing small financing costs therefore does not imply that financiers are weak.
4. **Prop. 3 (comparative statics).** Early financing becomes more attractive as access to alternative financing (γ) improves. Firms with good alternatives keep more slack, the opposite of search-friction models such as Hugonnier–Malamud–Morellec. Slack vanishes as θ → 1 or γ → ∞.
5. **Investment irreversibility (numerical, A ∈ [0.17, 0.18]).** With reversible investment, firms finance early and costs are small even at γ = 0. With irreversible investment, only productive firms, which can cut investment after a failed negotiation, finance early. Unproductive firms wait until funds run out and pay large rents. Irreversibility therefore raises financing costs disproportionately for low-productivity firms.

**Why it matters.** It offers a new microfoundation for economic inaction and cash holdings in which the costs of action are endogenous to strategy. It explains why large creditworthy firms hold a lot of slack, and it predicts that slack is non-monotonic in how specialized a firm's financing relationships are (VC stage specialists, sole lead arrangers).

**Dynamic games, contracts & markets link:** a dynamic bargaining game with endogenous outside options. The firm's state (its cash) sets its bargaining position, so the timing of trades and the division of surplus are jointly determined in equilibrium, a recurring-holdup problem.

# Frequent Stress Tests (Deepal Basak, Indiana Kelley; Mayur Choudhary, Indiana Kelley; Zhen Zhou, Tsinghua PBCSF)
*Draft of April 17, 2026*

**Question.** Silicon Valley Bank was not due for its next supervisory stress test until 2024 and failed in March 2023. When is a stress-test policy credible, meaning that even under the worst equilibrium no bank that passes a test later fails? And what role does test *frequency*, not just stringency, play?

**Model/approach.** A dynamic regime-change (bank-run) global game with incomplete information and asynchronous moves. A unit mass of creditors is split into N groups that act in sequence. Group n sees the first n public pass/fail results plus a noisy private signal. The regulator commits to a frequency N and a stringency k: the bank passes test n if its remaining fundamental can absorb a k-fraction attack by the agents yet to move. The analysis restricts to monotone strategies and uses adversarial equilibrium selection, in which the regulator plans for the worst equilibrium. An "obedient" equilibrium always exists, with survival threshold k. A policy is credible if and only if no "disobedient" equilibrium exists.

**Findings.**
1. **Thm 1.** Credibility holds if and only if Condition C holds: the marginal group-1 agent strictly prefers not to attack in any candidate equilibrium where group 1 is the only disobedient group. Sufficiency uses a "widening gap" property: under monotone play a stronger bank accumulates less attack over time.
2. **Thm 2.** Frequency and stringency are substitutes. Credibility requires k ≥ k(N), where k(N) is decreasing and discretely convex in N, so extra tests have diminishing value. The mechanism is that more frequent tests shrink the excess attack (x − k)/N that the first skeptical cohort can inflict before the next test reassures later groups.
3. **Prop. 4.** The credible set grows when private signals are noisier (higher σ) and shrinks when agents are keener to run (higher p).
4. **Thm 3 and Corollary 1.** With convex testing costs, the optimal credible policy picks a finite N_s and the least stringent credible k. Regulators should test more often and less stringently when shocks are more likely (π) and when a failure imposes bigger externalities (χ), which supports more frequent tests for systemically important banks.
5. **Extensions.** Prop. 5: a bank choosing its own tests prefers fewer than the planner. Prop. 6: by choosing its staggered debt-maturity structure M*, a bank can force the regulator to its privately optimal frequency, since tests between maturity dates add nothing. Prop. 7: a higher liquidity coverage ratio raises the optimal frequency and welfare, so liquidity regulation complements stress tests.

**Why it matters.** It turns frequency into an explicit policy lever. Regulators can buy credibility with more frequent tests instead of harsher scenarios, which lets more banks pass while keeping worst-case run risk under control. It nests Goldstein–Huang (2016) as the case N = 1 and Basak–Zhou (2020) as the case k = 0.

**Dynamic games, contracts & markets link:** this is dynamic information design aimed at a dynamic coordination game. What gets disclosed depends on the history of attacks, which creates a feedback loop between disclosure and play that the designer has to control under worst-case equilibrium selection.

# The Limits of Consumption Taxation: A Theory of Optimal Capital Taxation (Rafael Berriel & Ravi Jagadeesan, Stanford)
*Draft of July 16, 2025*

**Question.** Standard results (Kaldor, Chamley, Chari–Nicolini–Teles 2020) say capital should not be taxed, because consumption taxes can redistribute without distortion. That assumes consumption is observable. In reality, owner-managers disguise personal consumption as business expenses: Leite (2024), using Portuguese administrative data, finds new owner-managers shift 31% of household expenses to their firms. How does this imperfection change optimal capital and consumption taxation?

**Model/approach.** Dynamic mechanism design in continuous-time general equilibrium, building on DeMarzo–Sannikov (2006) and Di Tella (2019). Capitalist owner-managers face idiosyncratic profit risk the planner cannot verify. They can spend on productive intermediates or on disguised consumption, and the planner cannot tell the two apart. Hand-to-mouth workers earn wages. The planner maximizes discounted worker welfare subject to a promised utility for capitalists (as in Judd 1985). Tax instruments are unrestricted: the model decides which taxes are used. Capitalists have Epstein–Zin preferences (discount rate ρ, risk aversion γ, EIS ψ), and the planner's discount rate is ζ < ρ.

**Findings.**
1. **Thm 1 and Thm 3.** If the planner is patient enough, a steady state exists, is unique and is locally saddle-path stable, all in closed form. Capitalists' consumption as a share of capital is proportional to φ, their efficiency at disguising consumption. Capital sits strictly below the modified golden rule. If the planner is not patient enough, workers are immiserated in the long run.
2. **Thm 2.** The long-run savings wedge is positive and simple: ω = 2(ρ − ζ)/(ψ − 1). Sustained intertemporal distortion is optimal, overturning the zero-long-run-capital-tax result.
3. **Thm 4 (implementation).** Constant linear taxes on consumption, unexpected capital income and wealth implement the steady state, in closed form. Consumption and capital-income taxes are set so that the capitalist is indifferent at the margin between consuming openly and consuming through the business. A low disguise cost φ means a high consumption tax and a wealth *subsidy* (a patient planner raising long-run wages). A high φ means a lower consumption tax and a positive wealth tax.
4. **Numerical example.** With ρ = 6%, γ = 3, ψ = 3 and profit volatility σ = 35% (from Moskowitz–Vissing-Jørgensen), planner discount rates ζ ∈ (0, 4%) imply a steady-state capital-income tax of 30–60%, and 42.85% at ζ = 2%.
5. **Thm 5 and Thm 6 (verifiable total business expenses).** The qualitative conclusions survive, and one more distortion appears: the planner deliberately caps deductible business expenses to discourage disguised consumption.

**Why it matters.** It gives a clean, micro-founded reason for positive long-run capital taxation that is aimed at the wealthy, where precautionary-savings arguments for capital taxation don't apply. It also produces a tractable, implementable tax formula that ties rates to a measurable enforcement parameter.

**Dynamic games, contracts & markets link:** optimal taxation is solved as a continuous-time dynamic moral-hazard contract between the planner and capitalists, the same machinery as dynamic agency models of asset management, embedded in general-equilibrium market wages.

# Social Learning with Correlated Information (Yu Awaya, U Tokyo; Vijay Krishna, Penn State)

**Question.** More and more agents draw on the same information: reposts on X, AI systems trained on the same data, investors reading the same newsletters, news outlets recycling wire copy ("churnalism"). The ECB and IMF worry that this makes investment choices less diverse and creates systemic risk. In a herding model, does more commonality (serial correlation) of private information make an incorrect herd more likely, and does it make actions more alike?

**Model/approach.** The binary-state, binary-signal, binary-action sequential social-learning model of Banerjee (1992) and Bikhchandani-Hirshleifer-Welch (1992). The one change is that private signals follow a Markov chain given the state instead of being conditionally i.i.d. Equivalently, with probability p the next agent's signal is a copy of today's, and otherwise it is a fresh draw; p measures commonality. An outside observer who sees only actions forms "public beliefs." These are not Markov in general, but the authors show that in the candidate equilibrium they form a finite Markov chain with three absorbing (herd) states. A "reduction" lemma collapses signal histories to a few types. The martingale property of likelihood ratios then gives closed-form herd probabilities.

**Findings.** (1) *Theorem 1:* there is an essentially unique equilibrium, and a herd (possibly the wrong one) forms with probability one. (2) *Proposition 5.1:* a closed-form expression for the ex-ante probability of an incorrect herd, in terms of signal quality q, persistence, and two integer thresholds m and n. These are the numbers of identical actions needed to override one's own signal. (3) *Theorem 2:* the probability of an incorrect herd rises locally with persistence but has countably infinitely many downward jumps. Each jump happens when persistence gets high enough that one more identical action is needed to start a herd (m or n rises by 1). *Proposition 5.4:* its total variation is unbounded. Mechanism: when signals are more persistent, a run of L actions says less, because the predecessors' signals were likely copies. Agents therefore keep following their own signals for longer, which gives successors more information and delays herding. (4) *Proposition 5.3:* when q is close to the prior, some persistence strictly beats independent signals, so independence is not always best. At perfect persistence the function is discontinuous: the limit as persistence goes to 1 is strictly below the value at 1. (5) *Proposition 6.1:* more similar signals can make actions *less* similar, because a delayed herd means more actions still reflect noisy private signals. (6) "Anything goes": for high enough persistence, any finite action history that does not start with H is consistent with equilibrium. So behavior that looks "contrarian" (going against 10 prior L's) can be rational. Under i.i.d. signals only five patterns are possible.

**Why it matters.** It overturns the intuition behind policy worries about "information monoculture." Herding risk and similarity of actions are highly non-monotone in how correlated sources are, so the claim that common data sources cause correlated mistakes does not follow in general. The paper also offers a tractable model of correlated-signal herding, a case the literature (Smith-Sorensen, Kartik et al. 2024) has almost always assumed away.

**Dynamic games, contracts & markets link:** this is a dynamic game of sequential moves where history-dependent public beliefs drive behavior. The paper shows how the *serial structure* of information, not just its precision, shapes long-run outcomes. The authors motivate it with market settings: banks holding the same portfolios, and macroprudential concerns.

# Learning and the Emergence of Nonlinearity in Financial Markets (Ian Dew-Becker, Chicago Fed; Stefano Giglio, Yale; Pooya Molavi, Northwestern)

**Question.** Stock returns show a whole set of nonlinearities that are usually modeled one at a time: left skew and crashes; time-varying volatility that is itself positively skewed; the "leverage effect" (about −80% correlation between volatility changes and returns); a leverage effect whose strength varies with conditional skewness; and long memory, where volatility falls quickly after a spike and then slowly. Can one mechanism, investors learning about a latent fundamental, produce all of them together?

**Model/approach.** A general continuous-time filtering model. The log net present value of the market follows an essentially arbitrary (possibly jump or disaster) process. Agents see a Gaussian signal about it, and the price is their posterior mean. The core tool (Theorem 1) is a new cumulant recursion from nonlinear filtering. The sensitivity of the n-th posterior cumulant to news equals the (n+1)-th cumulant times signal precision. So the posterior mean responds in proportion to the posterior variance, and the variance responds in proportion to the posterior third cumulant. This also gives mean reversion in the variance. The result holds under misspecified priors and some behavioral updating errors (Section 5.1).

**Data/methods.** Calibration: fundamentals follow an i.i.d. disaster process with Barro-Jin (2011) jump sizes, the signal precision is chosen to match volatility, and moments come from 100-year simulated samples. Empirical tests use daily CRSP market returns with option-implied conditional volatility and skewness for the S&P 500. For contrast they use natural-gas futures and options (CME), which are positively skewed. The survey benchmark is the Yale Investor Behavior Project (1993–2024, 8,242 responses).

**Findings.** (1) Volatility equals posterior variance divided by signal noise, so uncertainty and volatility move together. (2) *Proposition 2:* the instantaneous regression coefficient of volatility changes on price changes equals κ3/(σY κ2). There is a leverage effect *if and only if* investors' beliefs about fundamentals are negatively skewed. This holds even when fundamentals have constant volatility. (3) Learning slows as uncertainty falls, which generically produces long memory: quick initial mean reversion in volatility, then slow. (4) The calibration, with random-walk fundamentals and no volatility dynamics of its own, matches the first four moments reasonably well. Daily return s.d. is 0.98 in the model vs 1.14 in the data. The correlation of volatility changes with returns is −0.76 vs −0.78. The model also matches skewness by horizon and volatility autocorrelations. After a negative jump, the average peak volatility over five years is 80% vs 37% without a jump, and volatility peaks about 9 months out. (5) Regression tests of the model-implied volatility law of motion. The coefficient on scaled skew×return should be 1: it is 1.01 (s.e. 0.03) for the S&P 500 and 1.13 (0.06) for natural gas. Quadratic mean reversion (vol²) beats linear mean reversion; for the S&P its t-statistic is about 40 times larger. (6) Model-free estimates: investors' posterior s.d. of fundamentals is 10.4–16.5%, and belief skewness is between −0.29 and −1.13. This is close to the 17.0% cross-sectional disagreement in the Yale survey.

**Why it matters.** The rich higher-order dynamics of returns do not need many separate mechanisms. Believing that crashes are possible is enough, even in samples with no disaster. The cumulant recursion is a general tool for non-Gaussian belief dynamics, which the authors suggest applying to inflation expectations (skewed, with volatility rising in the level).

**Dynamic games, contracts & markets link:** this is the session's macro-finance bridge. Dynamic belief updating in a competitive market, rather than strategic play, sets prices. The paper supplies a general theory of how posterior moments evolve, and several theory papers in the session (belief dynamics, misspecified learning) rely on similar recursive belief tools.

# Reputational Spillovers (Aditya Kuvalekar, Essex / visiting ISB; Anna Sanktjohanser, TSE)

**Question.** In bilateral reputational bargaining (Abreu-Gul 2000), a reputation for toughness pays. Many parties, though, bargain on several fronts at once: sovereigns with several creditor classes, firms with several unions, governments with several disputes. When concessions are public and one type governs behavior in every negotiation, does toughness still pay?

**Model/approach.** A three-player star. A center C bargains at the same time with peripherals A and B, each in a continuous-time Abreu-Gul war of attrition. Each player is either rational or a commitment type who insists on a fixed share. C's type is *global*, so a concession by C in one dispute shows C is rational in both. This creates a belief-consistency constraint across the two negotiations. The authors characterize the no-concession subgame through indifference conditions and complementary slackness, together with posterior-ordering lemmas that force all three posteriors to be equal once everyone is conceding.

**Findings.** (1) *Proposition 1:* there is a unique equilibrium with a sharp phase structure. There may be a time-0 concession atom. If one peripheral (A) is uniquely the most reputable, an initial phase follows in which A waits while B and C concede at hazards λ_B = (1+π)λ^AG and λ_C = λ^AG. Then all three concede at the bilateral Abreu-Gul rate λ^AG = r(1−α)/(2α−1) until a common finite terminal time. The atom is pinned down by requiring B's and C's posteriors to reach A's prior at the same moment. (2) *Proposition 2:* spillovers matter for payoffs *iff* a peripheral is uniquely most reputable at the start. If the center is weakly the most reputable, the outcome equals two independent Abreu-Gul games. If z_A > z_B ≥ z_C, the center's total payoff is unchanged, but the strong peripheral A strictly loses and the weak B strictly gains. If z_A > z_C > z_B, the center is *strictly worse off* than in two separate bilateral games, A loses, and B weakly gains. The weak peripheral gains more when the center's higher-stakes dispute is with A. (3) The mechanism: when A waits, C's concession would settle both disputes and give up the option value of holding out against A. To keep C willing to delay, B must concede faster than in isolation. The early mismatch is absorbed through time-0 atoms that shift toward C. (4) *Proposition 3:* the center's loss does not come from large commitment probabilities. As all priors vanish with fixed ratios, the payoff gap converges to a positive limit. (5) Extensions (Online Appendix). With sequential negotiations, C can be forced to concede an atom at time 0 even when all reputations are equal, which makes C's disadvantage sharper. Under partial observability, where the other peripheral sees only that a settlement happened, any agreement lowers the belief that C is committed, and C again concedes an atom. A numerical four-player star shows the same result: toughness is a liability for the center.

**Why it matters.** It gives a clean, uniquely determined multilateral reputational-bargaining model (the multiplicity problem noted by Fanning-Wolitzky 2022 goes away). Its predictions are testable: holdout creditors with small exposures can win outsized terms because the sovereign's big fight is elsewhere, and smaller unions benefit when management's key negotiation is with the larger union.

**Dynamic games, contracts & markets link:** this is a continuous-time dynamic game in which reputation is a state variable shared across linked contracts. It shows how public learning across markets changes the value of commitment and moves surplus toward the weaker parties.

# Dynamic Disclosure with(out) Timestamps (Aaron Kolb, Indiana Kelley; Beixi Zhou, Pittsburgh)

*Note: the PDF on Zhou's site is dated September 18, 2026. Its abstract is updated from the program version ("stock of undisclosed good evidence ... stochastically purged"; bad evidence "disclosed in bursts").*

**Question.** Some evidence carries a verifiable date (medical tests, audit reports, bankruptcy filings, forensic logs) and some does not (recycled news photos, sales "sandbagging" where closeable deals are pushed into the next period). How does the presence of a timestamp change when a sender chooses to disclose hard evidence about a changing state?

**Model/approach.** A continuous-time sender-receiver game without commitment. A hidden binary state θ_t follows a Markov chain with switching rates λ0 and λ1. At an exponentially distributed random time τ, the sender privately gets one piece of hard evidence showing θ_τ, and can disclose it at any later time. The sender's flow payoff is the receiver's belief that θ_t = 1, discounted at rate r. With timestamps, the receiver sees both θ_τ and τ. Without them, the receiver must infer τ. The receiver side is microfounded by a continuum of receivers with quadratic loss, which makes welfare comparisons possible.

**Findings.** (1) *Theorem 1 (timestamps):* there is a unique equilibrium. Good evidence is disclosed immediately, since delay only lowers beliefs while the evidence is withheld. Bad evidence dated s is disclosed after a deterministic delay a*(s), at the first moment the post-disclosure belief path crosses the no-disclosure path from below. Disclosing bad evidence is attractive because it proves the evidence is old, and it ends the "no news is bad news" drift. If the bad state is absorbing (λ0 = 0), bad evidence is never disclosed. The equilibrium does not depend on the sender's discount rate. *Proposition 2:* the Minimum Principle of Acharya-DeMarzo-Kremer and Guttman-Kremer-Skrzypacz holds. (2) *Proposition 3 (no timestamps):* if p0 > (λ0+r)/(λ0+λ1+r), every equilibrium builds an immediate stock of undisclosed good evidence. A sender can wait briefly and pass old good news off as fresh. *Lemma 1:* the stock must run down continuously by a finite time T, so a jump to full transparency (bang-bang) is ruled out. (3) *Theorem 2:* with an absorbing bad state, there is a unique three-phase equilibrium: *stockpiling* (no disclosure), *purging* (age-independent stochastic disclosure whose intensity rises and eventually explodes), then *transparency*. Beliefs are summarized by a few stock variables. (4) *Theorem 3:* with an impatient sender and a stationary prior, good evidence is disclosed immediately, while bad evidence comes out in isolated bursts. The gaps between bursts grow without bound as r → ∞ (*Proposition 5*). (5) Welfare: the sender is indifferent ex ante across equilibria by the law of iterated expectations (Lemma 4). Receivers, however, are better off with timestamps. The timestamps equilibrium Blackwell-dominates the three-phase equilibrium, and it beats the burst equilibrium when r is large. (6) A "first-in-first-out" (FIFO) equilibrium also exists (*Proposition 6*), but its transparency starts strictly later. The authors argue the three-phase equilibrium is the focal one.

**Why it matters.** It identifies a new institutional lever: requiring that evidence be timestamped (dated filings, audit logs, provenance metadata) speeds up good-news disclosure and makes bad-news disclosure possible. It also explains sandbagging and "burst" disclosure of old bad news as equilibrium behavior.

**Dynamic games, contracts & markets link:** this is a dynamic signaling game where the sender's "type" includes when the evidence arrived. Verifiability of timing, a feature of the contracting environment, changes equilibrium delay. That links information design to disclosure regulation in markets.

# Best Garbling is No Garbling: Persuasion in Real Time (Can Urgun, UNC; Mark Whitmeyer, Arizona State)

**Question.** In drug approvals, loan underwriting, jury verdicts, and tenure votes, a sender builds up evidence over time to push a receiver's belief past a threshold, and delay is costly. If the sender can both *garble* the flow of evidence (reduce its informativeness, stay silent, release batches) and choose *when to stop*, how should she use the two tools?

**Model/approach.** A binary state sets the drift of a Brownian evidence process dX = μdt + σdB, with signal-to-noise ratio κ = (μh−μℓ)/σ. The sender commits to any public reporting policy: continuous transforms plus independent noise, Poisson-marked messages, silence, delayed or batched disclosure. Assumption 2.1 rules out foresight of future evidence. She also picks a stopping time and pays a convex, increasing delay cost c(t). The receiver acts when her posterior crosses a threshold P̄. The policies considered are "clock-dominated": at every date the posterior equals a copy of the transparent posterior evaluated at some elapsed time no greater than calendar time. The main tool is Root's (1969) Skorokhod-embedding barrier.

**Findings.** (1) *Proposition 3.2 / Corollary 3.3:* for a given terminal distribution of posteriors, no public policy reaches it in less expected time than full transparency. Every transparent stopping rule that produces it has the same expected duration, 2/κ²·(E[Ψ(Q)] − Ψ(p0)). (2) *Proposition 3.5:* among transparent rules producing a given distribution, the Root barrier gives a stopping time that is convex-order smallest, so it is the least dispersed. (3) *Theorem 3.7 (main result):* any clock-dominated policy is weakly beaten by full transparency combined with a Root stopping rule. The terminal posterior distribution is the same, and the stopping time is smaller in the increasing-convex order. "No garbling is the fastest way to embed any target distribution." The improvement is strict when costs are strictly increasing and the policy is sometimes slower than real time, or when costs are strictly convex and the time distributions differ. Garbling increases time risk, which a convex cost penalizes. (4) *Theorem 4.6 / Corollary 4.7:* if the *receiver* chooses when to stop and the disclosure clock is "unbanked" (no bursts faster than real time), every receiver equilibrium has a transparent acceleration. The sender is weakly better off, and the receiver gains exactly her saved waiting cost κ_R·E[ρ − C_ρ]. (5) Comparative statics: higher incremental delay costs move both the earliest and latest optimal stopping rules earlier and lower the minimum and maximum persuasion probabilities (*Proposition 5.5*). A higher κ is just a time change, so it raises both persuasion probabilities (*Proposition 5.6*).

**Why it matters.** In real-time persuasion, all of the sender's leverage comes from controlling *when to stop*, never from degrading the evidence. That gives a sharp prediction and a normative argument for transparent evidence streams, for example in trial monitoring or rolling reviews. It also reconnects Kamenica-Gentzkow persuasion with Wald-style optimal stopping and Skorokhod embedding.

**Dynamic games, contracts & markets link:** this is a dynamic information-design problem in which the timing instrument dominates the informativeness instrument. It shows how commitment to a stopping rule is the relevant contract in dynamic persuasion. It fits alongside the session's other disclosure-timing papers (Kolb-Zhou, Brandl-Shi).

# Sequential Sampling under Adversarial Manipulation (Florian Brandl, Bonn; Xianwen Shi, Toronto)

**Question.** Wald's (1945) insight is that sequential sampling beats fixed-sample tests because you can stop as soon as the evidence is decisive. That assumes the evidence process is exogenous. What happens when an interested party (a manager on a trial run, a startup under VC monitoring, a firm rolling out a regulated product) can adaptively and secretly suppress bad news?

**Model/approach.** A continuous-time bad-news game. Decision-maker Alice samples a Poisson process at flow cost c. In the low state, breakdowns arrive at rate λ and reveal the state; in the high state, none arrive. Manipulator Bob always wants the high action. He can cut the breakdown rate to (1−β)λ at flow cost γβ, with c < γ < λ/2. Both start with the same uncertainty. Alice's stopping is public, and Bob's manipulation is hidden and can adapt over time. Bob's mixed strategies are Aumann-style (a randomly drawn pure strategy).

**Findings.** (1) *Theorem 1:* in every equilibrium, along the no-breakdown path Alice stops at a deterministic time t1. Bob mixes only between two strategies: never manipulate, or manipulate at full intensity from s1 until t1. He delays manipulation because early effort is wasted if a breakdown comes anyway. Equilibria fall into four classes: instantaneous deterrence, deadline deterrence, Wald, and manipulation. (2) *Theorem 2:* manipulation equilibria exist on an interval of priors with lower bound γ/λ. Below it, Bob's benefit p0(1−e^{−λt}) never covers the cost γt. Above a cutoff p*, the equilibrium starts with a passive period. (3) *Theorem 3:* for a given prior, all manipulation equilibria share the same s1 and t1, so multiplicity is limited to the mixing probability α. (4) *Theorems 4–5:* a no-manipulation Wald equilibrium coexists with them. Alice strictly prefers Wald, and Bob strictly prefers any manipulation equilibrium, so equilibrium selection is a coordination and commitment problem. If Alice can commit to stopping at the Wald time, Bob's manipulation is shut down. (5) *Proposition 1:* static sampling (commit to a sample size, Bob picks a suppressed subsample) deters manipulation. Alice sets the sample just long enough to make Bob indifferent. Static sampling can strictly beat the *worst* manipulation equilibrium under sequential sampling, reversing Wald's ranking. *Proposition 2:* a hybrid protocol, sequential exploration on [0, s] followed by a committed confirmatory sample on [s, t], can beat both pure methods. (6) The key ingredients are symmetric uncertainty and adaptive manipulation. If Bob knows the state, the unique equilibrium has Alice exit at time 0 (*Proposition 3*). If manipulation must be chosen and paid for up front, only the Wald outcome survives (*Proposition 4*). With slightly convex costs, Bob's effort ramps up but the structure is otherwise similar (*Proposition 5*).

**Why it matters.** It makes a design argument for pre-registration and fixed confirmatory phases in trials, audits, A/B tests, and regulatory rollouts. When the party being evaluated can suppress bad news, the flexibility of sequential testing is a weakness, and commitment has value.

**Dynamic games, contracts & markets link:** this is a stopping game with hidden adaptive actions, where the optimal "contract" is the sampling protocol itself. It shows how commitment (static or hybrid rules) disciplines manipulation in a dynamic principal-agent learning problem.

# Time Trumps Quantity in the Market for Lemons (William Fuchs, UT Austin & UC3M; Piero Gottardi, Essex; Humberto Moreira, FGV EPGE)
*Version read: January 3, 2024 draft (29 pp.) posted with the 2024 AEA/ASSA program.*

**Question.** In adverse-selection markets, a seller with a high-quality asset can signal it by *retaining* part of the asset (Leland-Pyle, DeMarzo-Duffie) or by *delaying* trade (Spence/Nöldeke-van Damme, Fuchs-Skrzypacz). Which instrument gets used when a seller of a perfectly divisible asset has both, facing competitive buyers over time?

**Model/approach.** A seller privately knows her cost/type c ∈ [0,1] of a divisible unit asset, buyers have a higher common value v(c), preferences are linear, and trade happens at a given set of dates (discrete with period Δ, or continuous). Three market arrangements are compared: (i) commitment to a long-term exclusive contract (path of trades); (ii) spot contracts with *publicly observed* past trades; (iii) spot contracts with *privately observed* past trades, where the seller's residual holdings become a second, endogenous dimension of private information. D1-type refinements select equilibria.

**Findings.** (1) *Commitment (Theorem 1):* all D1 equilibria fully separate, but only the discounted total quantity Q(c) = exp(−∫₀ᶜ v′(x)/(v(x)−x) dx) is pinned down; delay and partial sales are perfect substitutes, and welfare does not depend on how often trade can happen (unlike the indivisible-asset case in Fuchs-Skrzypacz 2019). (2) *Public spot trading (Proposition 1):* for every Δ and horizon there is a unique equilibrium. Each type trades in at most two consecutive periods and sells out, and every type gets exactly its commitment payoff. So spot markets with transparent histories lose nothing relative to commitment. (3) *Continuous-time limit (Theorem 2 and §4.2):* as Δ→0, each type sells its *entire* unit at one date τ(c) with e^(−rτ(c)) = Q(c). Only time is used to signal, and the limit is continuous. (4) *Private trading:* with discrete dates the public-trade outcome cannot be sustained (Proposition 2) because the two-price-per-quantity structure needs conditioning on history. With two dates there is an equilibrium with a pooling middle tier, where low types separate, intermediate types pool on selling everything in period 2, and high types never trade (Proposition 3). Welfare across the public and private regimes cannot be ranked in general. With continuous trading, private trades again deliver the commitment outcome through pure timing (Proposition 4, Theorem 3). (5) *Non-uniform supply (Proposition 5):* if supply s(c) rises with quality, low types cannot mimic high types' quantities, so trade is immediate and first-best under any regime.

**Why it matters.** The intuition is that time is irreversible and so works as built-in commitment: once you have waited, you have credibly waited. A promise to hold back part of the asset, by contrast, is never credible when gains from trade remain ("you will always succumb to selling the rest"). This overturns the quantity-retention intuition behind much of security-design finance. It also shows that trade observability and trading frequency interact, which matters for designing exchanges and post-trade transparency rules (e.g., TRACE-style disclosure).

**Dynamic games, contracts & markets link:** this is a clean case of a commitment-vs-spot contracting question in a dynamic signaling market. Spot markets with public histories replicate the commitment contract exactly, and the "right" dynamic signaling instrument is determined endogenously by what can be credibly committed to over time.

# Automation, AI, and the Intergenerational Transmission of Knowledge (Enrique Ide, IESE Business School)
*Version read: June 10, 2026 draft (51 pp.).*

**Question.** If AI automates entry-level work, does it erode the apprenticeship channel through which tacit knowledge passes from experts to novices? What does that imply for long-run growth and welfare?

**Model/approach.** Task-based overlapping-generations growth model. Agents live two periods, first as novices and then as experts. Expert skill is observable, but the knowledge behind it is tacit: it passes only by working together and is non-verifiable and non-pledgeable. Production combines expert skill with routine tasks done by machines (more productive, but only on automatable tasks) or by novices. This gives many-to-one matching: each producing expert hires N−I novices, who inherit the mentor's skill. Knowledge-transfer contracts are incomplete, and wealthless novices face a zero lower bound on wages. They can pay for mentorship only with current labor, not by borrowing against future expertise. The experts in each cohort split endogenously into inactive, price-cleared, and oversubscribed tiers (at the top, the wage ZLB binds and entry-level positions are rationed).

**Findings.** (1) *Growth (Propositions 1–3):* there is a unique dynamic equilibrium. With a fat-tailed (regularly varying) initial skill distribution, output grows asymptotically at 1+g = (N−I)^θ, set by experts' span of control and tail thickness θ, and normalized skills converge to a Pareto law. (2) *Automation shock (Proposition 4):* expanding the set of automatable tasks raises output on impact but permanently lowers the growth rate, so output eventually falls below the no-shock path (long-run reversal). The mechanism is *mentor composition*, not job loss: freed-up novices are reallocated to lower-skill experts, so best practices diffuse more slowly. (3) *Welfare (Proposition 5):* there is a threshold β* below 1/(1+g) such that laissez-faire adoption lowers welfare whenever the discount factor β exceeds β*, which means private adoption can be socially excessive. This happens only because the wage ZLB stops novices from paying elite mentors. (4) Countervailing *scale effect*: if automation saves enough supervision time that top experts expand their span, growth can rise. The paper argues that early generative-AI evidence (weaker junior employment in AI-exposed occupations) fits displacement better. (5) *Task-creating technologies (Proposition 6)* raise output, growth, and welfare unambiguously. Labor/capital-augmenting technologies raise growth only through scale effects. Extensions cover tasks with no learning value and attention dilution. Policy ideas include taxing or limiting entry-level automation tools, subsidizing junior positions with high-quality mentorship, and residency-style training support.

**Why it matters.** It gives a formal model of "seniority-biased technological change." A technology that complements expert judgment and substitutes for junior execution can erode the future supply of the expertise it complements. The model predicts that aggregate entry-level employment is the wrong statistic to watch: what matters is *who* juniors are matched with.

**Dynamic games, contracts & markets link:** the core friction is an incomplete, non-pledgeable dynamic contract (labor-for-training) in a matching market across generations. Technology changes the equilibrium assignment in that market, and the missing intertemporal contract turns a static efficiency gain into a dynamic welfare loss.

# Employer Competition and Certification (Hershdeep Chopra, Northwestern University)
*Version read: undated job-market-style draft (51 pp.).*

**Question.** When employers screen applicants through a for-profit third-party certifier (bar exam, USMLE, Freelancer/ExpertRating-style tests), how does the certifier's rent extraction feed back into employers' competition over hiring standards?

**Model/approach.** Three layers. (a) An applicant has binary ability and a privately known belief μ that he is high ability. (b) A revenue-maximizing monopoly certifier sells a menu of tests (Blackwell experiments) and faces a *joint* screening and information-design problem: IC, IR, and obedience constraints in a direct mechanism that recommends "top", "bottom", or "unemployed". (c) Two vertically differentiated employers (top gives more applicant utility, u < 1 for bottom) commit to hiring standards s_t, s_b (minimum posterior expected ability) before the certifier designs tests. The prior is below the hiring threshold, so without certification the market fails (Akerlof). The benchmark is a certifier that observes μ.

**Findings.** (1) Screening causes two distortions relative to the benchmark. The first is *exclusion*: low types are not sold tests, so unemployment rises and certification becomes a stronger barrier to entry. The second is *reduced informativeness*: the certifier pools more low-ability applicants into recommendations for the *less selective* employer, because this shrinks the heterogeneity of willingness to pay and therefore the information rents. (2) *Theorem 1:* whenever s_t > s_b, the optimal mechanism sends high-ability applicants to the bottom employer with positive probability, and for some parameters sends applicants only to the bottom employer. In the benchmark, the certifier would allocate only to the top employer for a range of those standards. (3) *Theorem 2 (uniform prior):* for an interval of parameters, the top employer *never* sets the maximal standard s_t = 1 in equilibrium, although the benchmark's unique equilibrium has s_t = 1. Screening frictions give even a "weak" bottom employer market power and narrow the gap between standards ("constriction of standards"). (4) Proposition 3: with u = 0 and a convex inverse hazard rate, the top employer's payoff rises in its standard and s_t = 1. (5) Extensions: with wages, flexible design lets the certifier pool until employers break even, so vertical differentiation alone produces no meaningful wage competition. If the certifier also charges employers a share of match surplus (a two-sided platform), tests can become more informative and the skew can go either way.

**Why it matters.** Regulating or reforming the upstream certification market (licensing, test pricing, platform fees) spills over into downstream labor-market competition in ways that are not obvious. The same logic applies to credit rating agencies and seller quality assurance.

**Dynamic games, contracts & markets link:** it is a contracting-and-markets paper rather than a dynamic one. It combines mechanism design (price-discriminating menus of information) with persuasion and oligopsony competition, and the equilibrium feedback between contracts (tests) and market structure (hiring standards) drives the result.

# What You Can't See: Contingent Thinking Failures in Dynamic Markets (Shani Cohen, Hebrew University)
*Version read: July 20, 2026 draft (48 pp.).*

**Question.** Lab evidence shows that people learn correctly from events that have *happened* but fail to reason about *hypothetical* events: limit orders vs. market orders (37% vs. 19% fail to learn from price in Ngangoué-Weizsäcker 2021), pivotal voting, winner's curse. What equilibrium concept captures this in competitive markets, and what does it imply for asset prices?

**Model/approach.** The paper defines *Dynamic Cursed Expectations* (DCE). Agents update correctly on observed fundamentals, signals, and *past* prices, and know the correct marginal distribution of each future variable. However, they treat events in different future periods as independent (the product of the marginals), so they do not anticipate learning from present or future prices. *DCEE* is the matching market-clearing equilibrium, a dynamic analogue of Eyster-Rabin-Vayanos cursed expectations equilibrium and a market counterpart of Cohen-Li's Sequential Cursed Equilibrium. Existence is proved (Theorem 3.6) for financial economies with CARA traders, riskless storage, and "imminent" private information (signals only about the next dividend). The application is a multi-asset mean-variance economy with IID normal dividends and N traders, compared with REE. A partially cursed χ-DCEE blends the two.

**Findings.** (1) Failing to learn from the *present* price causes overtrading: DCEE has trade where REE has none (no-trade theorem). Expected per-agent volume is derived in closed form (Proposition 4.3); it rises with the number of distinct signals and falls with dividend uncertainty. (2) Failing to anticipate learning from *future* prices makes traders ignore the positive correlation between tomorrow's resale price and the following dividend. They underestimate variance, so risk aversion leads to excess risk-taking. (3) *Proposition 4.1:* the expected price bias p^DCEE − p^REE declines over time. It is *negative* at the last trading date (undervaluation), and whenever dividend variance σ² ≥ signal noise τ², assets are overvalued at every earlier date for any N, with the bias growing in σ² and in the remaining horizon. (4) In χ-DCEE the bias is exactly χ times the full-DCEE bias. The paper argues the concept applies to mortgages (fixed vs. variable), irreversible investment, and inflation forecasting.

**Why it matters.** It provides a portable, empirically grounded alternative to rational expectations. Agents are not naive about data (they know the marginals and learn from history) but do not understand temporal correlation. This yields a new source of overpricing and excess volume that does not need disagreement or short-sale constraints.

**Dynamic games, contracts & markets link:** it brings a behavioral solution concept from extensive-form games (sequential cursedness) into dynamic competitive markets, showing that how agents fail to reason about hypothetical future histories changes equilibrium prices and volume.

# Implicit Incentive Provision with Misspecified Learning (Federico Echenique, UC Berkeley; Anqi Li, University of Waterloo)
*Version read: undated draft (68 pp. including online appendices).*

**Question.** In a career-concerns setting, what happens when society (evaluator plus market) holds a *dogmatic, wrong* belief about an agent's innate ability, for example a stereotype, but learns about the productivity of effort from outcomes? How do misspecified learning, the evaluator's assessment intensity, and the agent's effort feed back on each other?

**Model/approach.** Holmström (1999) single-period career concerns with three players. An evaluator commits to an assessment intensity h (lower noise, at cost κ(h)). The agent chooses effort a. The market rewards perceived ability η plus a valuation of effort v_M. Outcome X = η + r(a,β) + ε, with Var(ε) = 1/h − 1. Society's prior on mean ability is degenerate and off by Δ, and its prior on effort productivity β has full support. The solution concept is Berk-Nash equilibrium (Esponda-Pouzo): beliefs concentrate on KL minimizers given the behavior they induce. Local asymptotic stability selects among equilibria, with learning foundations in an online appendix (stochastic approximation, where society's posterior converges a.s. to stable-equilibrium beliefs). Applications are education (school as evaluator) and entry-level labor (manager appraisals).

**Findings.** (1) *Theorem 1A (underestimated ability, Δ < 0):* there is a unique, stable Berk-Nash equilibrium. Better-than-expected outcomes are misattributed to *higher* effort productivity (β̂ > β*), which raises assessment and effort. Beliefs and assessment are substitutes, so the distortion is O(Δ). (2) *Theorem 1B (overestimated ability, Δ > 0):* productivity is underestimated, assessment falls, and beliefs become more pessimistic. Complementarity produces an odd number of equilibria that alternate stable and unstable, possibly including a "cascade" to the lower bound of the support even when misspecification is small. (3) *Theorem 2:* the stable-equilibrium distortions rise in the weak set order with |Δ| and with weaker assessment, and only these comparative statics have learning foundations. (4) *Proposition 1 (discrimination):* with identical groups where society overrates m and underrates w, society overestimates w's productivity and scrutinizes w more harshly. w truly works harder, but the *perceived* effort gap is even larger, so the stereotype spreads from the ability domain to the effort domain. If only ability is rewarded, w earns less despite more effort. If effort is also rewarded, the reward gap can narrow or reverse, which can *look like* equity while stereotypes are entrenched. (5) Policies that intensify assessment (teacher pay-for-performance, curriculum alignment, better assessment technology, lower effort costs) reduce distortions. Color-sighted affirmative action, with equal assessment intensity and group-specific beliefs, puts a lower bound on assessment and stops the belief cascade (Theorem 3).

**Why it matters.** It explains how ability stereotypes can persist and reinforce themselves through the endogenous evaluation and incentive environment, not just through direct taste or statistical discrimination. It also warns that narrowing outcome gaps is not evidence that bias has gone.

**Dynamic games, contracts & markets link:** it combines implicit (career-concerns) incentive contracts with long-run misspecified learning. The steady states of a dynamic learning process between an evaluator, an agent, and a market decide both incentive provision and which equilibrium society ends up in.

# Strategic Learning with Asymmetric Rationality (Qingmin Liu, Columbia; Yuyang Miao, Columbia)
*Version read: arXiv 2510.23951v2, November 10, 2025 (47 pp.).*

**Question.** How should a boundedly rational decision-maker (FDA, SEC, a busy policymaker, a voter) learn from a fully rational, biased expert who controls the flow of evidence? What is the cost of guarding against manipulation?

**Model/approach.** The sender privately knows a binary state and always wants action H. Each period she decides whether to generate an i.i.d. public signal from a fixed, unfalsifiable process (she can stop but not fabricate). The receiver wants to match the state and is modeled, as in Hellman-Cover (1970) and Wilson (2014), as an optimally designed *finite automaton* with m memory states (transition rule, initial distribution, action rule). There is no discounting, so stopping is not driven by impatience.

**Findings.** (1) *Theorem 1:* any protocol can be weakly improved by a **parsimonious** one, with two absorbing states for the two final decisions and all transient states prescribing the sender-unfavorable action. The sender therefore keeps providing signals (Lemma 3), as if non-strategic, since stopping early guarantees her worst outcome. (2) *Theorem 2:* the receiver's optimal payoff is 1 − 2√(p(1−p))·(γ^((m−2)/2)−1)/(γ^(m−2)−1) when γ^(m−2) > κ (γ measures the spread of signal likelihood ratios). This is exactly the Hellman-Cover payoff with **m−1** states against a non-strategic sender. The cost of strategic manipulation is exactly one memory state. If γ^(m−2) ≤ κ, the receiver just acts on the prior. For m ≥ 4 the supremum is not attained. In the symmetric binary case the payoff is q^(m−2)/(q^(m−2)+(1−q)^(m−2)) (Corollary 2). (3) Standard non-strategic optimal automata, with recurrent extreme states, are *fully manipulable*: a strategic sender gets H with probability one. (4) *Theorem 3:* the sender's limiting payoff is p + (2p−1)/(γ^(m−2)−1) (formula reconstructed from garbled PDF math extraction — verify against the paper before citing). A sender with an unfavorable prior wants more informative signals, and one with a favorable prior wants less (Corollary 4). (5) *Theorem 4 (behavior):* in optimal sequences, absorption happens only from the penultimate state on an extreme signal and with vanishing probability. This gives **indecision near the decision point** (many confirming signals needed, and one disconfirming signal causes regression), **information disengagement** once a view is formed, and **polarization** (the same signal history can lead to opposite absorbed views).

**Why it matters.** Behaviors usually labeled psychological biases (tuning out media after deciding, last-minute doubt, negativity bias, entrenched opposing opinions from identical information) emerge here as optimal responses to strategic information provision under memory limits. The paper also gives a procedural-rationality view of how regulators should design rules for evaluating evidence.

**Dynamic games, contracts & markets link:** it is a dynamic persuasion and stopping game in which the uninformed side designs the "contract" (a finite-state decision protocol) to provide incentives. It shows that commitment to absorbing, irreversible decisions is the incentive device, which parallels the session's themes of commitment and dynamic information provision.
